# Agent studios land first and judge later

The teams running the largest fleets of coding agents have settled on one shape. A coordinator or planner pushes narrow units to agents, and each agent owns an isolated checkout. A single mechanical landing point serializes what reaches main. The only hard check in front of main is a fast automated one, and human judgment moves after the merge. Lauren Tan's agents merge their own PRs while she reviews "what they landed on main when i was away or asleep." OpenAI's harness team runs "minimal blocking merge gates," and its lead says "most of the human review is post merge." Steve Yegge abandoned his bisecting merge queue for a "Land Rush" that slams 120 to 150 commits onto main and fixes red main by swarm diagnosis. Where a gate survives, an agent holds it, not a human. pstack is Tan's published playbook. Its landing playbooks will not merge without a verdict from an agent that did not write the code. The verifier runs on a different model family, and its verdict is bound to the exact head SHA. The binding constraint in every system, agent or human, is queue arithmetic: gate latency times commit rate. Teams that kept a gate made it fast: OpenAI's build runs in under a minute, and Google runs only fast tests before submit and the rest after. Teams that could not make the gate fast dropped it and scheduled a reconciliation pass instead. For Herdr Brain, the evidence supports landing first and reviewing once after merge. It also shows what every working post-merge regime adds: evidence bound to the landed SHA, an independent reviewer on a different model, findings filed as prioritized new work, a fast revert path, and tests that every change must extend. Almost all of the evidence is self-reported throughput. No source publishes escaped-defect or revert rates for its fast-landing regime.

## Cursor moved the merge gate from humans to non-author agents

Lauren Tan (poteto), a Member of Technical Staff at Cursor, publishes her method as pstack, a plugin in Cursor's public plugins repository. She calls it "the same skills i use every day to ship high quality code at Cursor," and it promises "fearless parallelism" once an agent can be trusted to "write good, verifiable code" ([pstack README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/README.md)). On 2026-10-26 it stood at version 0.15.32 with commits through 2026-10-23. Its landing playbooks were last rewritten on 2026-10-14, when a commit dropped Graphite from the orchestration playbook and "cut playbooks to principles" ([pstack history](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/commits/main/pstack)). It is the most detailed public specification of a multi-agent landing pipeline. It also takes the opposite emphasis to Herdr Brain's current policy: humans leave the per-PR gate entirely, but agents do not.

### pstack pushes briefs to single writers and binds every verdict to a SHA

Work in pstack is pushed, never pulled. The Orchestrate playbook covers multi-day programs of "dozens to hundreds of subagents," with a human checking in twice a day. Its coordinator "own[s] the program, never the code": it writes briefs, drains a queue and keeps "the frontier green," and it "never authors or edits code." Conflicted merges and chain rebases are tasks in their own right. Mechanically landing a verified unit by fast-forward or clean cherry-pick is the one piece of git bookkeeping the coordinator may do itself ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). Every brief must name:

| Field | What it carries |
|---|---|
| GOAL | What the unit is for |
| SCOPE | The paths it may write, and "its exclusive worktree or branch" |
| CONTEXT | Upstream reports pasted in full, "because workers cannot see siblings" |
| ACCEPTANCE | What counts as done |
| VERIFY | The exact commands to run |
| TIMEBOX | How long it may take |
| FORBIDDEN | Banned actions: "no PR retargets or merges, no rebase, no force-push" |
| REPORT | A format that includes the head SHA and "what you actually ran" |
| Standing orders | Pasted verbatim |

**"Missing fields are a refuse-to-spawn condition"** ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). Workers run in cloud VMs by default, with "One writer per worktree or branch." The hierarchy stops at coordinator, track and worker. About ten children run at once as "a rolling window. Never as blocking batches, which cost the slowest child of every batch." Hard-coded swarm trees "were tried and parked as too rigid." Before scaling, a pilot pushes one unit "through the whole path… while that costs one agent instead of fifty" ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)).

The coordinator's durable store works like an event-sourced record. Every file has exactly one writer, and `units.tsv` holds each unit's id, state, branch, PR and head SHA. The status page is "derived from `units.tsv` and `ledger.tsv` at each drain, never hand-maintained." A CLI manages the store under a pid lock and writes atomically through a temporary file and rename. "The CLI never spawns, waits, or wakes anything" ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md); [orch.ts](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/scripts/orch/orch.ts)). Completions "are queue events, not interrupts." The coordinator records an inbox pointer, returns to its work and drains completions in batches. **"Never review a diff inside a drain"**: a completion that needs review becomes a verifier unit. Liveness is checked read-only through side effects, and "Transcript mtime is not liveness." Retries depend on how the run failed and are capped at "Two retries, then abandon the unit and replan around it." Agents that return late are reconciled against the current frontier, "never a blind merge" ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)).

The gate before main is an agent verdict, recorded in a ledger keyed by PR number and head SHA. The levels are `live-ui-verified`, `unit-test-verified`, `type-check-only`, `verifier-blocked` and `verifier-failed`. **"CI green is an input to a verdict, not a verdict,"** and **"A new head SHA voids the row"** ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). The verifier runs "on a different model family from its worker." By default, Grok 4.7 handles workers and verification, and Claude Opus 5.5 takes the hardest changes and judgment calls ([poteto-mode SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/SKILL.md)). Verification is scaled to the unit. When VERIFY is a single cheap command, the worker reports its own result and the coordinator spot-checks the receipts, because "A verifier agent whose entire product would be rerunning one command is ceremony, not verification" ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)).

The Autopilot-full playbook covers runs where agents have authority to land. One cloud owner agent carries each PR from build to merge, "in parallel off trunk, never stacked, so each PR lands on its own." A full verification swarm runs **once, at the code-ready head**: it re-runs the gates, proves the behavior live on the real surface and audits the diff. A later rebase, conflict resolution or minor fix gets a single verifier at the new head. Proven findings go back to the owner as one fix-forward "with a red test across every site with the same defect." A defect that trunk already has "is recorded against trunk and does not gate the PR" ([autopilot-full.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/autopilot-full.md)). A failed verification "gets a fix unit, not a re-verify" ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)).

Collisions are handled by giving each role one owner, not by locks. "Exactly one stacker per stack may mutate topology," workers "never rebase and never retarget or merge PRs," and merges are "units with briefs like any other" ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). Rebases are kept to a minimum because "each rebase restarts CI." Just before merge, the owner judges how far trunk has drifted. It rebases only for a conflict, a file both sides changed, or a change to which checks run. If trunk changed a caller or dependency of the patch, it runs the patch's checks on the merge result. "Otherwise merge." If trunk would force a third rebase, the owner holds and reports ([autopilot-full.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/autopilot-full.md)).

Shipping a stack lands "only the contiguous verified run rooted at the bottom," one squash merge at a time from the bottom up. Each PR is read back afterwards, because "a merge command can report success when a merge queue only queued the PR." A verdict survives a rebase when `git patch-id` shows the patch changed only in tests, docs or lint config ([shipping.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/shipping.md)). A CI failure blamed on flakiness or infrastructure earns "one fresh build, never a job retry… An identical second failure means it was never flake." Bugbot, Cursor's AI code reviewer, "is triaged skeptically, always": real findings are fixed red-first, noise is dismissed with a concrete disproof, and "Never churn code to quiet a bot" ([babysit.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/babysit.md); [bugbot-triage.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/references/bugbot-triage.md)).

After merge, "One retro watcher follows merged PRs for reverts, post-merge CI breaks, and orphaned follow-ups" ([orchestrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). Human review becomes an audit of decisions. An overnight run logs each decision with its reason and an evidence pointer, and `/show-me-your-work` "spawns a reviewer on a different model family to read the trail and the transcript." In the playbook's words: "You're auditing decisions, not re-reading the whole night" ([07-overnight.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/docs/guide/07-overnight.md)). Humans stay in front of main for five things: irreversible actions, approvals the forge enforces, items the operator names, interaction-changing PRs in the multi-phase template, and the whole landing in the Autopilot-stack variant ([autopilot-full.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/autopilot-full.md); [multi-phase-plan.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/multi-phase-plan.md); [autopilot-stack.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/autopilot-stack.md)). The precondition is stated outright: "Earn the trust before the loop." That means doing the task once by hand, giving the agent your verification tools, letting every stage "stop the line," and turning repeated failures "into tools, skills, or checks" ([07-overnight.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/docs/guide/07-overnight.md)).

### Tan's own words confirm self-merging agents; the rest is secondhand

Her own words confirm only two mechanics. On 2026-08-11 she wrote: **"because of Dune, my agents now merge their own PRs. i do code review now by looking at what they landed on main when i was away or asleep"** ([X post, 2026-08-11](https://x.com/poteto/status/2087244771849089270)). On 2026-09-21 she posted a video on "how i shipped 2,500 PRs last month to production" ([X post, 2026-09-21](https://x.com/poteto/status/2102050467505430555)). Cursor Compile London had billed the talk as "I Shipped 2,000 PRs Last Month" ([Cursor Compile London](https://cursor.com/compile/london)). Both posts were read through X's oEmbed endpoint, because x.com itself refused the fetch.

Everything else comes from write-ups of recordings this research could not fetch, so it should be read as secondhand. A workshop write-up reports about 20 PRs auto-merged overnight, review comments converted into CI rules, and CI bans on `useEffect` and on ordinary code comments in "Dune," an Electron architecture built to "make the shortest path the correct one" ([xiaohu.ai](https://xiaohu.ai/en/article/cursor-agent-team-24x7/)). A write-up of a 2026-10-02 livestream reports more than ten coordinators acting "like chiefs of staff" and review as "rigorous sampling after merge," with faulty changes reverted; it also relays her remark about cutting verification cost by "reducing 10 verifiers to 1," or by having the implementer verify its own work ([atlasnote.ai](https://atlasnote.ai/en/articles/poteto-pstack-ai-agent-verification)). A third write-up describes a "trust ladder" for where to encode a correction, running from codebase changes and static analysis, which are durable, down to rules, skills and style guides, which are weak ([ratstack.sh](https://ratstack.sh/lore/poteto-lauren-tan-2500-prs-dune)). One careful reviewer notes that the "chief of staff" pattern does not appear in pstack and cannot be attributed to her ([folkfox](https://folkfox.com/pstack-2500-ai-agent-prs-verification/)). Taken together, the sources suggest a split. Dune's hard CI constraints carry the code-quality load that review comments once carried, and verification agents check behavior. No primary source says whether her own runs use Autopilot-full's full swarms or a cheaper self-verification variant.

### Cursor's research harness abandoned locks, the integrator and per-commit correctness

Wilson Lin's two posts give the clearest published account of what breaks at scale. In the first design, equal agents claimed tasks from a shared status file with locks. They "would hold locks for too long, or forget to release them entirely," and **"Twenty agents would slow down to the effective throughput of two or three."** Optimistic concurrency was "simpler and more robust," but without a hierarchy the agents "became risk-averse" and made only small, safe changes ([Cursor: Scaling long-running autonomous coding](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). A second design, with an upfront planner, workers and a judge, was "bottlenecked by the slowest worker." A third, a continuous executor with no planner or judge, became overloaded, skipped merges and claimed it had finished early ([Cursor: Towards self-driving codebases](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)).

The design that worked uses recursive planners that do no coding. Each worker works "in its own repo copy," never talks to another worker, and writes one handoff upward with "notes, concerns, deviations, findings." An integrator was added to serialize pushes, rebases and merges. It became "an obvious bottleneck" and was removed ([self-driving codebases](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)). Requiring 100% correctness before every commit also serialized the system, because one API change or typo stalled everyone. Lin accepted **a "small and constant" error rate**, fixed forward by other agents. He paired it with a "green" branch that an agent snapshots periodically and fixes up before release ([self-driving codebases](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)). Runs peaked at about 1,000 commits per hour, with several hundred agents per machine. Disk traffic from each agent's own build artifacts became the bottleneck ([self-driving codebases](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)).

Simon Willison interviewed Lin about the browser run. It peaked near 2,000 concurrent agents and almost 30,000 commits, with activity logs showing pushes straight to main. "Most commits do not have merge conflicts" because the planner divides scope, and requiring every commit to compile would be "a synchronization bottleneck" ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/jan/23/fastrender)). Lin also found that "constraints work better than instructions," citing "No TODOs, no partial implementations," and that Opus 4.5 "tended to stop early and take shortcuts" on long autonomous work ([scaling agents](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents); [self-driving codebases](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)).

### Cursor's products keep bot review advisory and hook passes after merge

Cursor's shipped products mirror the research. Projects (2026-09-10) provide a coordinator that "doesn't write code itself but directs other agents," runs cloud subagents in parallel on parts of a feature, and adds "a lint rule whenever it sees the same mistake twice"; in migrations, "Early on, you review each PR closely," and later "you review less" ([Cursor: Projects](https://cursor.com/blog/projects)). Bugbot runs on every PR update, but its findings default to a **`neutral` check result that does not block merges** unless it is configured to fail. Its configuration is read from the base branch "so a PR can't change how it reviews itself," and its learned rules are mined from merged or closed PRs ([Cursor docs: Bugbot](https://cursor.com/docs/bugbot)). Automations can trigger on "A PR is merged" and "A GitHub check finishes," which is the product hook for a post-merge agent pass, although Cursor documents no post-merge review example ([Cursor help: Automations](https://cursor.com/help/ai-features/automations)).

## Anthropic and OpenAI put the verifier, not the reviewer, in front of main

### Sixteen Claudes shared one bare repo, claimed work by lock file, and had no review

Nicholas Carlini's C compiler project (2026-02-05) is the purest flat swarm on record. Sixteen Opus 4.6 agents each ran in a Docker container, inside a bash `while true` loop that kept restarting Claude Code, and each cloned a shared bare repository mounted at `/upstream`; "I don't use an orchestration agent." An agent claimed work by committing a text file such as `current_tasks/parse_if_statement.txt`, and when two agents claimed the same task, "git's synchronization forces the second agent to pick a different one." A finished agent pulled from upstream, merged, pushed and removed its lock: "Merge conflicts are frequent, but Claude is smart enough to figure that out" ([Anthropic: Building a C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). There was no review of any kind. The run went "without active human intervention," and the repository warns: "None of it has been validated for correctness" ([C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler); [GitHub: claudes-c-compiler](https://github.com/anthropics/claudes-c-compiler)). The gate was the test suite: **"it's important that the task verifier is nearly perfect, otherwise Claude will solve the wrong problem."** The suite drew on established compiler test suites, open-source projects' own build and test scripts, and new tests for failure modes seen during the run. The compiler reached 99% on most suites ([C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)).

Two failures reshaped the harness, and both fixes changed the environment, not the coordination. First, Claude would "frequently break existing functionality each time it implemented a new feature." A CI pipeline was added late so that new commits "can't break existing code." Second, on the Linux kernel, "Every agent would hit the same bug, fix that bug, and then overwrite each other's changes." The fix was to make the verifier divide the work. GCC served as a known-good oracle that compiled most kernel files, and delta debugging isolated the files where Claude's compiler failed, so agents could fix different bugs in parallel ([C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)).

The harness also gave each agent a `--fast` mode that ran a 1% or 10% sample of the tests. The sample was "deterministic per-agent but random across VMs," so the fleet as a whole covered the full suite without any one agent paying for it ([C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). The project took nearly 2,000 sessions over two weeks, just under $20,000 and 3,982 commits. It produced a 100,000-line Rust compiler that boots Linux 6.9 on three architectures ([C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler); [GitHub](https://github.com/anthropics/claudes-c-compiler)).

### Anthropic's harness guidance turns "done" into a file agents may not weaken

Justin Young's guidance (2025-11-26) concerns one agent working across many context windows, not concurrency. It still establishes the artifact that later systems reuse. An initializer session writes an `init.sh` script, a `claude-progress.txt` log, an initial commit and a JSON list of over 200 features, "all initially marked as 'failing'." Coding agents may change only each feature's `passes` field, because **"It is unacceptable to remove or edit tests."** JSON was chosen because the model "is less likely to inappropriately change or overwrite JSON files compared to Markdown files" ([Anthropic: Effective harnesses for long-running agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). Each session reads the Git log and the progress file, runs an end-to-end smoke test before touching code, and takes one feature. It finishes by leaving "the kind of code that would be appropriate for merging to a main branch." The failure mode was agents marking features complete without testing them. Prompting them to test through browser automation fixed it ([effective harnesses](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)).

Prithvi Rajasekaran's follow-up (2026-03-24) adds a separate evaluator. When agents grade their own work, they "tend to respond by confidently praising the work," and tuning a skeptical evaluator proved more tractable than making the generator self-critical. Generator and evaluator agree a sprint contract with hard thresholds before any code is written ([Anthropic: Harness design for long-running application development](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). As models improved, he removed the sprints and replaced per-sprint evaluation with **a single pass at the end**. His principle: "every component in a harness encodes an assumption about what the model can't do on its own." An evaluator "is worth the cost when the task sits beyond what the current model does reliably solo." The full harness cost about 20 times as much as a solo run: 6 hours and $200, against 20 minutes and $9 ([harness design](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)).

### Claude Code ships claiming, worktrees and a review that never blocks

Claude Code's built-in tools encode the same lessons. `/batch` "has Claude split one large change into 5 to 30 worktree-isolated subagents" ([Claude Code docs: Run agents in parallel](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agents)). The experimental agent teams share a task list in which "Task claiming uses file locking to prevent race conditions," blocked tasks cannot be claimed, and dependent tasks unblock automatically; but teammates get no worktree isolation, so "Two teammates editing the same file leads to overwrites," and the docs recommend three to five teammates while warning that "Task status can lag" ([Claude Code docs: Agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)). A `TaskCompleted` hook that exits with code 2 can "prevent completion and send feedback," a done-gate on a work item that is separate from any merge gate ([agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)). Worktrees branch fresh from the remote default branch by default, block edits and Git redirects into the main checkout, and hold a `git worktree lock` while the agent runs ([Claude Code docs: Worktrees](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/worktrees)). Code Review runs several agents over the diff, adds a verification step that filters out false positives, and reads `REVIEW.md` from the base branch, "never from the pull request's own changes"; it **"never approves or blocks the pull request,"** and it reads code without building or running it ([Claude Code docs: Code Review](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review)).

### Anthropic's own process hard-gates in CI and samples automated approvals

Jason Clinton's account of Anthropic's internal process (2026-07-21) is the most quantified production report in this set. "Claude authors about 80% of the code merged into our codebase today," and "More than half of all code is being merged by our internal version of Claude Tag." Engineers ship eight times as much code per quarter as they did in 2021 to 2025. The share of PRs with substantive review comments rose from 16% to 54% ([claude.com: How Anthropic secures its AI-native SDLC](https://claude.com/blog/how-anthropic-secures-its-ai-native-software-development-lifecycle)).

The controls are layered rather than concentrated at merge. The team chose to put its **"hard code review gate at the test/CI stage,"** where several narrowly scoped review agents must each "write a proof that their finding is valid." Entire codebases keep strict human approval, tiered by risk, and humans sample "a percentage of all automated approvals." New AI reviewers run in shadow mode, posting comments for human approval "until trust is earned," and after deploy come dynamic security scanning, a bug bounty and red teaming ([Anthropic SDLC](https://claude.com/blog/how-anthropic-secures-its-ai-native-software-development-lifecycle)). This is the clearest production example of "gate cheaply in CI, audit a sample afterwards."

### OpenAI bet on sub-minute builds, agent review and cleanup after merge

Ryan Lopopolo's essay "Harness engineering" (2026-02-11) returned HTTP 403 on every fetch. Its quotations here come from a secondary wiki and InfoQ, cross-checked against Lopopolo's own Latent Space interview. A team of three engineers, later seven, had Codex write about a million lines over five months with no hand-written code. They merged about 1,500 PRs at 3.5 per engineer per day ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex)), a rate Lopopolo later put at "five to 10 PRS per day per engineer" ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/harness-eng)).

Search snippets of the essay give its gate in one passage: "The repository operates with minimal blocking merge gates. Pull requests are short-lived. Test flakes are often addressed with follow-up runs rather than blocking progress indefinitely" ([OpenAI, via search snippet](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)). The merge philosophy, per the secondary copy: **"In a system where agent throughput far exceeds human attention, corrections are cheap, and waiting is expensive."** The essay adds that this "would be irresponsible in a low-throughput environment." Humans "may review pull requests, but aren't required to." Almost all review effort has moved to being "handled agent-to-agent," and "Most refactoring PRs can be reviewed in under a minute and automerged" ([AI wiki, secondary](https://businessdatasolutions.github.io/ai-wiki/sources/2026-02-11-lopopolo-codex-harness-engineering)). Lopopolo's own words are stronger: **"Most of the human review is post merge at this point."** The team "had to retool the entire build system to complete in under a minute," and "We make very heavy use of work trees" across a repository built as "like 500 NPM packages" ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/harness-eng)).

Landing runs through a `$land` skill. It coaches Codex to push the PR, wait for human and agent reviewers and green CI, fix flakes, resolve upstream conflicts and enter the merge queue ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/harness-eng)). A transcript summary adds two tuning details. Reviewer agents were told to "bias toward merging" and to raise only high-severity issues. Authoring agents were allowed to push back, because they had been following review feedback too readily ([you-tldr transcript, secondary](https://you-tldr.com/transcript/CeOXx-XTYek)). Humans do not repair bad PRs: "The elixir service will completely trash the entire work tree and start it again from scratch" ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/harness-eng)).

The constraints are mechanical. AGENTS.md is about 100 lines and points into a structured `docs/` tree whose cross-links are enforced by linters and CI. Dependencies must flow "Types → Config → Repo → Service → Runtime → UI," and structural tests check that they do. Custom linters' error messages include remediation instructions, so each violation teaches the agent how to fix it ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex); [engineering.fyi, secondary](https://www.engineering.fyi/article/harness-engineering-leveraging-codex-in-an-agent-first-world)).

The post-merge loop is explicit "garbage collection." "Codex replicates patterns that already exist in the repository — even uneven or suboptimal ones." The team first spent every Friday, 20% of the week, cleaning up, and that did not scale. Recurring background Codex tasks replaced it. They scan for deviations from "golden principles," update quality grades and open small refactoring PRs that are automerged ([AI wiki, secondary](https://businessdatasolutions.github.io/ai-wiki/sources/2026-02-11-lopopolo-codex-harness-engineering)). A human-approved smoke test still gates promotion to distribution. The first month and a half was "10 times slower" than Lopopolo working alone ([you-tldr, secondary](https://you-tldr.com/transcript/CeOXx-XTYek)).

Symphony, an OpenAI project Lopopolo links to, is **the closest published analogue to a model-free dispatcher**. Its draft specification polls a Linear board every 30 seconds, dispatches issues by priority and then age, and runs at most 10 agents at once by default (`max_concurrent_agents`). Each issue gets a persistent workspace; a stalled run is killed after five minutes and retried with exponential backoff capped at five minutes; state lives in memory with "No durable database"; and ticket and PR writes are left to the coding agent while the service stays "a scheduler/runner and tracker reader" ([Symphony SPEC.md](https://github.com/openai/symphony/blob/main/SPEC.md)). Agents provide "proof of work: CI status, PR review feedback, complexity analysis, and walkthrough videos," and then "land the PR safely" ([GitHub: openai/symphony](https://github.com/openai/symphony)). Codex's GitHub reviewer "flags only P0 and P1 issues" and reads its review rules from the AGENTS.md closest to each changed file. Its docs state that review rules "don't replace tests, branch protections, or required approvals" ([Codex docs: GitHub](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/third-party/github)).

## Independent orchestrators learned that serial integrators break under load

### Gas Town pushes beads onto hooks and gives one Refinery all merging

Steve Yegge's Gas Town (launched 2026-01-01) is push-based like pstack. Work items are "beads." The Mayor, "your concierge and chief-of-staff," starts most convoys of work, and `gt sling` puts a bead on an agent's hook under the propulsion rule "If there is work on your Hook, YOU MUST RUN IT" ([Welcome to Gas Town](https://yegge.ai/essays/welcome-to-gas-town/); [gastown glossary](https://github.com/gastownhall/gastown/blob/main/docs/glossary.md)). Polecats are short-lived workers whose sandbox is a git worktree on a branch cut "from current main"; on `gt done`, a polecat pushes its branch, files a merge-request bead, clears its hook and ends its own session ([polecat-lifecycle.md](https://github.com/gastownhall/gastown/blob/main/docs/concepts/polecat-lifecycle.md)).

Yegge's reason for a dedicated merger is the clearest statement of the swarm-merge problem. Workers "get into a monkey knife fight over rebasing/merging," and "The baseline can change so much during a swarm that the final workers getting merged are trying to merge against an unrecognizable new head." The Refinery is "the engineer agent responsible for intelligently merging all changes, one at a time, to main. No work can be lost" ([Welcome to Gas Town](https://yegge.ai/essays/welcome-to-gas-town/)).

It later became "a Bors-style merge queue — polecats never push directly to main." The queue batches pending merge requests, rebases them as a stack and tests the tip. On green it fast-forwards all of them; on red it bisects to find the failing one. The gates it runs are pluggable ([gastown README](https://github.com/gastownhall/gastown); [architecture.md](https://github.com/gastownhall/gastown/blob/main/docs/design/architecture.md)). The architecture doc still lists batch-then-bisect as blocked behind an in-progress phase, so the docs disagree about whether it has shipped ([architecture.md](https://github.com/gastownhall/gastown/blob/main/docs/design/architecture.md)).

**When a rebase fails, "Refinery creates a conflict-resolution task"** for another polecat; it does not bounce the work back to the original worker ([polecat-lifecycle.md](https://github.com/gastownhall/gastown/blob/main/docs/concepts/polecat-lifecycle.md)). An epic lands as a unit through an `integration/<epic>` branch, which lands to main once all its children close. That prevents the case where one child "lands Wednesday, breaks A's work" ([integration-branches.md](https://github.com/gastownhall/gastown/blob/main/docs/concepts/integration-branches.md)). Beads, the shared work store, provides `bd ready`, which lists unblocked tasks, and `bd update <id> --claim`, which "Atomically claim[s] a task." Its hash IDs "prevent merge collisions in multi-agent/multi-branch workflows." Since v1.0 it has run on Dolt. The earlier SQLite-plus-JSONL version suffered "last-write wins semantics… race conditions, and tombstone hell" ([beads README](https://github.com/gastownhall/beads); [Clown Show to v1.0](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/gas-town-from-clown-show-to-v1-0-c239d9a407ec)).

Review in Gas Town mostly happened after merge: "regular code review sweeps, followed by bug-fix sweeps that fix the issues filed during the code-review sweeps… until the code reviews are just nitpicking" ([Gas Town Emergency User Manual](https://yegge.ai/essays/gas-town-emergency-user-manual/)). As a maintainer handling about 50 contributor PRs a day, Yegge added an explicit "Merge-fix" outcome: "We merge the PR as-is, then push a follow-up fix to main." Requesting changes is "the last resort." A patrol merges easy wins every two hours, and he merges about 88% of incoming contributor PRs ([Vibe Maintainer](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/vibe-maintainer-a2273a841040)).

The failures were in the supervisors and the model, not the merge mechanics. The Deacon, a patrol agent, was **"cleaning up 'stale' workers that weren't stale and murdering entire crews mid-task,"** and twelve days in Yegge had merged "44k lines of code that no human has looked at" ([Emergency User Manual](https://yegge.ai/essays/gas-town-emergency-user-manual/)). The Witness, which watches polecats, was single-threaded and processed "Only one completion… per cycle" of 30 to 90 seconds, until a redesign let polecats nudge the Refinery directly while the Refinery kept polling beads as the reliable path ([polecat-self-managed-completion.md](https://github.com/gastownhall/gastown/blob/main/docs/design/polecat-self-managed-completion.md)). The Mayor suffered weeks of "massive data loss" ([Clown Show to v1.0](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/gas-town-from-clown-show-to-v1-0-c239d9a407ec)). Finally, "Gas Town fell apart at the seams with Opus 4.7," whose "just two more things" tic "prevented Opus from ever converging" ([The Shape of Things to Come](https://yegge.ai/essays/the-shape-of-things-to-come/)).

### Wheelhouse traded the bisecting queue for a megabatch Land Rush

Yegge's newer private orchestrator, Wheelhouse, keeps the shape but moves review inside each unit. "Crew" agents running Fable produce designs and bead plans. A "fleet" of long-lived Opus 5 agents, each with its own clone, implements them. "Every implementation bead goes through this lifecycle: Fable design, Opus implementation, Fable review." The operating rule is "crons watch, models act," across about 45 launchd and systemd units, including "the Portcullis land queue" ([The Shape of Things to Come](https://yegge.ai/essays/the-shape-of-things-to-come/)).

Then the queue arithmetic broke: "my build gate… takes right around half an hour. So with 40+ agents around the clock, my MQ was growing without bound, shooting right past 100 MRs… We would get caught up in bisection loops." His fix: **"whenever the MQ hits 100, we abandon the bisection and just smash it all in with a megabatch. And then we do swarm diagnosis (not bisection) to fix it."** That cleared batches of 120 to 150 commits ([Shape of Things](https://yegge.ai/essays/the-shape-of-things-to-come/)). The queue has carried up to 500 commits a day and averages 270 ([Fences, not Sandboxes](https://yegge.ai/essays/fences-not-sandboxes/)).

The second failure was accumulated rules. Wheelhouse "accumulated over 400 ruling/law beads, 185 rule rows in CLAUDE.md alone, and 650 distinct refusal sites across 173 scripts. Before too long, no work was legal… it was nobody's job to curate or review the fences." Cutting them to 14 fences, with Yegge personally approving any new one, restored 250 to 300 meaningful commits a day ([Seats and Sunsets](https://yegge.ai/essays/seats-and-sunsets/)). The same essay names "a CLI orchestrator like Herdr" as part of the standard kit. On review, his forecast is to "keep reviewing agent code" for now, but "get ready to replace your human code review with many, many rounds of agentic code review" ([Shape of Things](https://yegge.ai/essays/the-shape-of-things-to-come/)).

### Steinberger went from one shared folder to worktrees plus autoreview

Peter Steinberger's 2025 practice was the minimalist pole. He ran 3 to 8 Codex sessions, "most of them in the same folder," committing straight to main. He had "experimented with worktrees, PRs but always revert back to this setup as it gets stuff done the fastest" ([Just Talk To It](https://steipete.me/posts/just-talk-to-it/)). He managed collisions with prompts and discipline. A `/commit` prompt told each agent that "multiple agents work in the same folder and to only commit your changes." A "blast radius" rule allowed "many small bombs" or one "Fat Man," never several large ones ([Just Talk To It](https://steipete.me/posts/just-talk-to-it/)). He limited the claim himself: "I usually work alone, if you work in a bigger team that workflow obv won't fly" ([Shipping at Inference-Speed](https://steipete.me/posts/2025/shipping-at-inference-speed/)).

By October 2026 he was at OpenAI and maintaining OpenClaw, a project with several maintainers, and his published rules reverse the 2025 approach. Agents create "task-owned Git worktrees… whenever useful." "Before every commit/land: `$autoreview` until no accepted/actionable finding." "PR CI is the default broad proof." Failed tests are rerun locally, "never a local full suite," and `gh run rerun --failed` is allowed only for a diagnosed infrastructure or flaky failure, "never to chase green" ([steipete/agent-scripts AGENTS.MD](https://github.com/steipete/agent-scripts/blob/main/AGENTS.MD)).

His maintainer-orchestrator skill lets investigation, implementation, local tests and review run in parallel. It **serializes "only outward-facing actions"**: pushes to the same branch family, PR updates, merges and releases. Landing requires "exact-head CI green" and a fresh autoreview. Each external wait has one owner, so the root "does not duplicate polling" ([maintainer-orchestrator SKILL.md](https://github.com/steipete/agent-scripts/blob/main/skills/maintainer-orchestrator/SKILL.md)). OpenClaw's own rules add "don't rebase or merge `main` just because it advanced" and "Prior-head CI stays prior-head evidence." Its review bot reviews each PR once, and that review is advisory ([openclaw AGENTS.md](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw/blob/main/AGENTS.md)).

### Smaller tools split on who folds results into main

The remaining tools differ mainly in who folds results in. Geoffrey Huntley's Ralph is one process doing "one task per loop," fanning out subagents for search but using "only 1 subagent for build/tests," and committing and pushing only when tests pass; Huntley rejects multi-agent designs as "a red hot mess" ([Ralph](https://ghuntley.com/ralph/)). Every's Compound Engineering plugin runs "parallel waves" of fresh workers one dependency layer at a time, after checking file paths, interfaces, migrations and shared resources; then **"The host alone applies, verifies, and commits"** each result, "one at a time against the advancing canonical tree," because "A clean patch is not proof of semantic compatibility" ([ce-work guide](https://github.com/EveryInc/compound-engineering-plugin/blob/main/docs/guides/ce-work.md)). Its multi-persona review only reports, so a reviewer never changes a checkout another agent is testing ([ce-code-review guide](https://github.com/EveryInc/compound-engineering-plugin/blob/main/docs/guides/ce-code-review.md)). Jesse Vincent's Superpowers runs a fresh implementer per task, a spec-and-quality review after each task, and a whole-branch review at the end ([superpowers SKILL.md](https://github.com/obra/superpowers/blob/main/skills/subagent-driven-development/SKILL.md)). Jeffrey Emanuel's Agent Mail uses voluntary file leases with an expiry time, enforced by a pre-commit guard ([mcp_agent_mail](https://github.com/Dicklesworthstone/mcp_agent_mail)). StrongDM, per Simon Willison, forbids both human-written and human-reviewed code and validates against holdout scenarios stored outside the codebase ([Simon Willison: StrongDM](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)). Willison himself names the human ceiling: "I can only focus on reviewing and landing one significant change at a time" ([Simon Willison: parallel coding agents](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Oct/5/parallel-coding-agents/)).

## Human practice already solved the queue arithmetic and post-merge review

### Merge queues hide serial latency with speculation, bisection and conflict analysis

Every merge queue enforces one invariant: main advances only to a commit that passed the required checks as merged with everything ahead of it. The queues differ only in how they hide the latency of a serial queue ([Cipriani, "It's still not rocket science"](https://tylercipriani.com/blog/2022/02/17/its-still-not-rocket-science/)). Uber's SubmitQueue paper supplies the arithmetic that makes hiding necessary: at 1,000 changes a day and 30-minute builds, **a serial queue makes the last change wait "over 20 days."** Batching "masks intermediate changes that fail," and the chance of conflict rises "from 5% to 40%" as concurrency grows. Uber's answer was a conflict analyzer that lets changes touching disjoint build targets commit in parallel, plus a predictive model that picks which speculative builds to run ([Uber EuroSys'19 slides](https://sundaram.io/slides/eurosys19.pdf); [Colyer, The Morning Paper](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/04/18/keeping-master-green-at-scale/)). Before SubmitQueue, Uber's iOS mainline was green only 52% of the time ([Colyer](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/04/18/keeping-master-green-at-scale/)).

GitHub's queue tests each PR "when applied to the latest version of the target branch and any pull requests already in the queue," with up to 100 concurrent builds and groups of 1 to 100 PRs; when a group fails, the failing PR is removed and the entries behind it are rebuilt without it ([GitHub Docs: merge queue](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/configuring-pull-request-merges/managing-a-merge-queue)). GitHub's own monorepo merges about 2,500 PRs a month from 500+ engineers through it, average wait fell by 33%, and its stated principle is **"The throughput of the overall system should be favored over fairness to an individual pull request"** ([GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/engineering/engineering-principles/how-github-uses-merge-queue-to-ship-hundreds-of-changes-every-day/)). Zuul tests changes speculatively in parallel (A alone, B on top of A, C on top of A+B) inside a window that starts at 20, grows by one per merge and halves on each failure ([Zuul docs: Project Gating](https://zuul-ci.org/docs/zuul/latest/gating.html)). Shopify tolerates flaky tests by ejecting a PR only after its fourth consecutive failure ([Shopify v2](https://shopify.engineering/successfully-merging-work-1000-developers)), and keeps branches short-lived by refusing PRs that drifted too far from their merge base ([Shopify v1](https://shopify.engineering/introducing-the-merge-queue)). Graphite's stack-aware queue validates a whole stack in parallel and fast-forwards without rerunning CI already validated "against that exact change" ([Graphite merge queue docs](https://graphite.com/docs/graphite-merge-queue)); Graphite has since joined Cursor ([Graphite blog](https://graphite.com/blog/graphite-joins-cursor)). Bors, archived in 2024, merged batches on a staging branch and split a failing batch in two until it isolated the culprit ([bors-ng](https://github.com/bors-ng/bors-ng)), and Rust still hand-assembles "rollups" of several PRs because its CI takes about three hours ([Rust Forge: Rollups](https://forge.rust-lang.org/release/rollups.html)).

### Trunk-based development caps branch life and now names agents

DORA, Google's DevOps research program, links higher delivery performance to "Three or fewer active branches," "Merging branches to trunk at least once a day" and "No code freezes and no integration phases." It recommends builds that finish "within a few minutes," with failures fixed quickly or reverted. It warns that "Heavy code review" pushes developers toward large batches ([DORA: Trunk-based development](https://dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/)). trunkbaseddevelopment.com caps a branch's life at about two days, and **"Time spent waiting for review counts towards the branch's short life"** ([TBD: Short-lived feature branches](https://trunkbaseddevelopment.com/short-lived-feature-branches/)).

The site now treats "one developer and their coding agents" as a single unit and calls for AI-led review. The reviewer should be "a reviewing agent that is independent of the agent that wrote the change… a separate session, with its own instructions, and perhaps a different model." "A co-author reviewing their own work is not a review." Humans "sample and spot-check what the AI-led review passed," and "AI-led review should take minutes or less" ([TBD: Continuous Review](https://trunkbaseddevelopment.com/continuous-review/)). DORA's 2024 report is the main caution. As AI adoption rose, delivery throughput fell by an estimated 1.5% and stability by 7.2%. The authors point back to "small batch sizes and robust testing mechanisms" ([Google Cloud: 2024 DORA report](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/announcing-the-2024-dora-report)).

### Post-merge review works only when findings carry obligations

Post-merge review is an old, documented practice. Ship/Show/Ask's "Show" option merges without waiting for feedback but still waits for automated checks, on the argument that "Code review, or 'Approval', should not be a requirement for a Pull Request to be merged" ([Ship / Show / Ask](https://martinfowler.com/articles/ship-show-ask.html)). Apache's commit-then-review lets committers change code "at will," but a technically justified veto forces the change to be reversed and "may not be overridden" ([Apache Foundation glossary](https://www.apache.org/foundation/glossary.html)). LLVM allows review "either before it is committed or after," holds that **"The bar for post-commit feedback is not higher than for pre-commit feedback,"** expects authors to "respond promptly," and says that on "substantial post-commit review feedback, please revert and address said feedback before recommitting" ([LLVM Code-Review Policy](https://llvm.org/docs/CodeReview.html); [LLVM Developer Policy](https://llvm.org/docs/DeveloperPolicy.html)).

Thierry de Pauw's case study supplies the operating mechanics. A team reviewed every commit after it reached mainline, using a "To Review" Kanban column checked at the start of each day, with commits grouped per feature. **"Any issue raised during a code review had to be picked up immediately with high priority."** The preconditions were an automated test for every change and static analysis that broke the build. Internal auditors judged that the team did more quality assessment than the standard process, with "even better audit tracking" ([de Pauw, Non-Blocking Continuous Code Reviews](https://thinkinglabs.io/articles/2023/05/02/non-blocking-continuous-code-reviews-a-case-study.html)).

Chromium is the counter-case. On 2021-03-24 it removed committers' ability to bypass review and OWNERS approval through TBR ("to be reviewed"), because the bypass affected "the integrity of Chrome source code and artifacts." A bot still exempts clean reverts and benign files from review, but never grants OWNERS approval ([Chromium docs: Mandatory Code-Review](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/docs/code_review_owners.md)). Google, the best-measured shop, keeps review before commit but makes it cheap. The median change is 24 lines, over 80% of changes have at most one reviewer, and median review latency is under four hours ([Sadowski et al., Modern Code Review at Google](https://research.google/pubs/modern-code-review-a-case-study-at-google/)).

For agents, a 2026 study of 9,799 agent-authored PRs reviewed by humans found that only 35.7% of rejections showed clear agent failure; 31.2% were driven by workflow constraints ([Peralta et al., MSR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22534)). No controlled study compares defect outcomes of post-merge review against pre-merge review.

### Google runs slow tests after submit and rolls back first

Google's continuous integration system, TAP, handles "more than 50,000 unique changes" a day. Before submit it runs "only fast, reliable" tests, because a change that passes them has "a very high likelihood (95%+) of passing the rest of the tests." All affected tests, including large ones, run asynchronously after submit, where "you can accept longer times and some instability" ([Software Engineering at Google, ch. 23](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch23.html)). When a post-submit batch fails, TAP splits it and reruns each change, or developers binary-search it.

The norm is rollback. "Rolling a change back is often the fastest and safest route," and a forward fix is "a riskier proposition." TAP can "automatically roll back changes when it has high confidence that they are the culprit." Google "strongly discourages committing any new work on top of known failing tests." Each team's Build Cop keeps its tests green "regardless of who breaks them" ([ch. 23](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch23.html)). Flaky tests dominate the noise: about 84% of observed pass-to-fail transitions involved a flaky test ([Google Testing Blog: Flaky Tests](https://testing.googleblog.com/2016/05/flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we.html)). Google has since developed flake-aware culprit finding, a Bayesian method that runs at milestone commits ([Flake Aware Culprit Finding](https://research.google/pubs/flake-aware-culprit-finding/)).

Google's site reliability engineers state the norm as "rolls back first and investigates the problem second" ([Google Cloud: CRE rollbacks](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/gcp/reliable-releases-and-rollbacks-cre-life-lessons/)). LLVM prefers "revert to green" over "fixing forward," while accepting that "it's okay to break the build occasionally" ([LLVM Developer Policy](https://llvm.org/docs/DeveloperPolicy.html)). Meta lands a change after running a model-selected third of the dependent tests, which catches "more than 99.9 percent of all regressions." It still runs "exhaustive testing before it is deployed" ([Meta: Predictive test selection](https://engineering.fb.com/developer-tools/predictive-test-selection/)), and uses kill switches as the escape after landing ([Meta: Rapid release at massive scale](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)).

### Stacks and tests grow by rule, not by threshold

Stacked diffs make each slice of a feature independently landable and revertable. Google sizes a changelist (CL) at about 100 lines. It requires each CL to "include related test code," and dependent CLs must keep "the whole system… working after each CL is submitted" ([Google Eng Practices: Small CLs](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)). Graphite lands a stack from the bottom up and waits for checks at each step ([Graphite docs: Merge pull requests](https://graphite.com/docs/merge-pull-requests)). Meta's Sapling rebases the rest of a stack automatically after an edit ([Meta: Sapling](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/11/15/open-source/sapling-source-control-scalable/)).

In the human literature, test suites grow through rules and feedback, never through a coverage gate. LLVM requires "test cases for any bugs fixed and any new features added," reduced so they do not bloat time-to-test ([LLVM Developer Policy](https://llvm.org/docs/DeveloperPolicy.html)). Google **"does not enforce any code coverage thresholds across the entire codebase"**; it shows coverage of each change during review and offers voluntary levels instead ([Code Coverage at Google](https://research.google/pubs/code-coverage-at-google/)). It also mutates only changed code during review and posts surviving mutants as findings with a "Please fix" button, after filtering out uninteresting "arid" code cut unproductive mutants "from 85% to 11%" ([Practical Mutation Testing at Google](https://research.google/pubs/practical-mutation-testing-at-scale-a-view-from-google/)). A growing suite stays trusted because highly flaky tests are quarantined with a filed bug ([Google Testing Blog](https://testing.googleblog.com/2016/05/flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we.html)), and stays affordable because test selection runs only the likely-relevant tests on each change ([Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/developer-tools/predictive-test-selection/)). For agents, which absorb findings cheaply, the same mechanics apply with one caution from LLVM: tests that bloat runtime lengthen every queue cycle.

### Seven patterns recur

**Decomposition is the product, so push beats pull at scale.** pstack says "The brief is the product." Yegge says polecats "shouldn't have to make decisions." Cursor's planners divide scope so that conflicts stay rare. Pull-claiming works when the units are already decomposed and the claim is atomic, as in Beads' `--claim`, Claude Code's file-locked task list and Carlini's git-committed lock files. It degrades when agents must also choose what to work on: Cursor's peer agents turned risk-averse, and Carlini's agents all chose the same kernel bug.

**Every writer gets its own checkout.** That means a worktree, a VM or a container. By 2026 every source isolates writers this way, including Steinberger. The two exceptions are a single-process loop (Ralph) and Claude Code's agent teams, which have to partition files by hand.

**One owner, acting mechanically, writes topology and history.** Examples are Gas Town's Refinery, pstack's stacker, Compound Engineering's host checkout and Steinberger's serialized outward-facing actions. Cursor's lesson draws the boundary: an integrator that also exercises judgment becomes "an obvious bottleneck," while a fast-forward or clean cherry-pick stays bookkeeping.

**Conflicts become new work, and rebases are rationed.** Gas Town turns a failed rebase into a conflict-resolution task. pstack rebases only for conflicts, shared files or changed checks. OpenClaw refuses to rebase "just because it advanced." OpenAI trashes a bad worktree and starts again.

**Evidence binds to an exact SHA.** pstack's ledger row is voided by a new head SHA. Steinberger requires exact-head CI. OpenClaw says "Prior-head CI stays prior-head evidence." Graphite skips CI already validated against the exact change. Binding evidence to a SHA is what lets review run asynchronously without becoming stale.

**The reviewer is independent by construction.** It is not the author. It runs on a different model family (pstack, Wheelhouse, Compound Engineering's adversarial lens, TBD.com). It reads its rules from the base branch, not the change (Bugbot, Claude Code's REVIEW.md). It is biased toward merging and raises only high-severity findings (Codex, OpenAI). It must prove each finding (Anthropic), and it is itself trusted only after a period in shadow mode.

**Failures become structure, and structure needs a curator.** Examples are OpenAI's linters with remediation messages, Cursor's lint rule after a mistake is seen twice, Tan's review comments turned into CI rules, Carlini's tests for observed failure modes, and Google's mutants posted as review findings. Yegge's 650 refusal sites show the other end of the same pattern: rules that nobody owns eventually make "no work legal."

### Four disagreements remain open

**Does a per-change gate exist at all?** pstack keeps a strict agent verdict on every PR. Anthropic keeps a hard gate in CI. OpenAI keeps a minimal gate: sub-minute CI and a merge queue. Cursor's research harness has no per-commit gate, and Yegge's Land Rush abandons the gate whenever the queue passes 100. The split follows the queue arithmetic and the product's stakes. Shipped products keep a fast gate. Throughput-maximizing systems drop the gate and add a reconciliation pass: Lin's green branch or Yegge's swarm diagnosis. No source drops the gate and also drops reconciliation.

**Is red main fixed forward or rolled back?** The agent-native sources fix forward: Cursor accepts a "small and constant" error rate, Yegge diagnoses red main by swarm, and OpenAI says "corrections are cheap." The established human sources roll back first: Google calls a forward fix "a riskier proposition" and TAP auto-reverts, LLVM prefers "revert to green," and DORA says fix quickly or revert. Agents make fixing forward cheaper. Small, independent commits make rolling back cheaper still. Google's rule against "committing any new work on top of known failing tests" has no counterpart in the agent sources.

**How much review, and where?** The answers range from none (Carlini, StrongDM), to once at the code-ready head (pstack), to a single pass at the end of a run (Anthropic's harness), to many rounds per step (Yegge's "Rule of Five," in which each step is reviewed four times, and his "many, many rounds of agentic code review"), to sampling after merge (Tan, Anthropic's internal process, OpenAI). The cost evidence leans toward fewer, better-placed reviews: Anthropic's harness author dropped per-sprint evaluation as models improved, and pstack treats a verifier that only reruns one command as "ceremony." Chromium's reversal marks a boundary every side respects in some form. Paths whose integrity depends on a second identity's approval stay gated before merge: pstack pauses for irreversible writes and never bypasses forge-enforced approvals, Anthropic keeps strict human approval for risk-tiered codebases, and OpenAI keeps a human-approved smoke test before distribution.

**Where does integration live, and who rebases?** Cursor removed its integrator. Gas Town and Compound Engineering are built around one. Steinberger's 2025 shared folder had no isolation at all, and by 2026 he had abandoned it. The mechanical-versus-judgment boundary reconciles most of this, but not all of it: Yegge's Refinery "intelligently" merges, which is judgment, and his own Witness and Deacon failures show what happens when supervisors judge.

The evidence behind all four positions is thin in the same way. Every throughput figure is self-reported: Tan's 2,500 PRs, OpenAI's 5 to 10 PRs per engineer per day, Anthropic's 8x, Yegge's 270 commits a day. No source publishes revert rates, escaped-defect rates or the cost of post-merge correction for its fast-landing regime.

## Conclusion

The research reframes "should main be gated?" as "where does each check's latency land, and who owes what when it fails?" Every system that outran one CI cycle per change did one of three things. It made the gate sub-minute (OpenAI). It moved slow checks after submit, running them at milestone commits with culprit finding (Google). Or it dropped the gate and scheduled a reconciliation pass (Cursor's green branch, Yegge's swarm diagnosis). Herdr Brain's current posture belongs to the third group: self-landing without a pre-main gate, red main accepted and fixed forward, and one full test run before release. The sources say that posture works only alongside its other half. That half is a model-free "Build Cop" that runs the suite at milestone commits, attributes red builds with flake awareness, and then reverts the culprit or files a fix item to its owning lane. It also includes a rule against stacking new work on a known red. A lane-local, targeted, sub-minute check recorded as producer evidence is not a gate the service waits on. What it does is raise the pre-land pass rate, and every batching and speculation scheme in the merge-queue literature depends on that pass rate. Two published supervisor failures transfer directly to a serial line service. Gas Town's Witness processed one completion per 30-to-90-second cycle, so every other completion waited behind it. Its Deacon reaped workers that were still alive. Symphony and Yegge's "crons watch, models act" show the service owning claiming, dispatch, fast-forwarding, queueing and side-effect liveness, and never owning judgment.

A review that never blocks is well supported. LLVM, de Pauw, OpenAI and Tan all make it work, but only where the review creates obligations and its independence is built into the structure. For Herdr, that means keying each review to the immutable landed commit and running it on a different model family from the author. The reviewer reads its rules from main, not from the change, requires proof for each finding and raises only P0 and P1 issues. Substantial findings default to revert-and-reland, as in LLVM, and minor ones become a linked follow-up. The reviewer itself is sampled, as in Anthropic's shadow mode and TBD.com's spot checks. Tests keep growing when every real finding lands with a red test (pstack) and recurring findings harden into lint or structural checks whose messages say how to fix them (OpenAI). Mutants on changed lines can also become review findings (Google). Yegge's 650 refusal sites carry a warning for an orchestrator whose rules grow from postmortems: each new check needs one curator and a high bar for admission, or the factory eventually makes "no work legal."
