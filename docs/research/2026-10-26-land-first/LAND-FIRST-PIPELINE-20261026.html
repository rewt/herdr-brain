Land First Pipeline

The engineering is fast. The time goes into the order of steps around it. A worker takes about 18 minutes and its code now reaches main about 8 minutes after it starts. But ready work sat with no engineer on it for 54 of the last 95 hours on Brain and 39 on BCH. Most blocked runs were not blocked by engineering. Everyone we studied who runs many agents at once has converged on one shape. We already have most of its parts; they are in the wrong order, with extra gates between them.

1 · Adopt the target flow below as the design

It's a reordering, not new machinery. Land first and evaluate after. The service lands, checks main and queues reviews; sessions only build and judge. Nothing is filed. If you agree, one owner builds it in the phases at the bottom, starting with the config-only fixes.

Recommend: yes

2 · What happens when main breaks

Today's rule is "red main accepted, fix forward". None of the established practices do that alone. Even the fastest agent shops pair fast landing with an automatic check after merge that finds the change that broke main and takes it back off. My proposal: the service runs the full suite on main after landings. When it finds the breaking change, it reverts it with a new commit on main and files a fix item to that change's owner. The fix lands as a fresh change. The original commit, its review and its evidence stay in the history, so nothing is lost and main is never red for long.

Recommend: automatic revert plus a fix item to the owner

The target flow

The only gates before main

  • One item has one claimed worker and lane at a time.
  • The real repository and worktree are checked, never a tab or label.
  • The lane is clean, the rebase has no conflicts, and main fast-forwards under one landing lock. The landing checks the claim's current ownership number and reads main fresh.
  • Project HOLD and STOP.

The worker runs the tests it touched and records the result as evidence. It isn't a gate the service waits on.

Splitting one item across engineers

  • A planner session splits the item into child items, each naming the files or areas it will touch.
  • The service runs the children in parallel, one lane each. They land in whatever order they finish.
  • Overlapping areas run one after another, or as an ordered chain of small changes that land bottom-up.
  • A landing conflict goes back to that worker as a rebase. It is never an escalation.
  • When the last child lands, a whole-feature check runs on main.

Tests and review that don't block

  • Every change ships its own tests; the brief says so.
  • Main health runs the full suite. Flaky tests are set aside automatically, each with a fix item.
  • Each landed change gets one review. It runs on a different model, is tied to the exact commit and flags only serious issues. A Major reverts the change to its lane; smaller findings become follow-ups.
  • A sample of the reviewer's verdicts is checked each week.
  • Outward steps keep their checks where the action happens: publishing, funds and secrets.

Who has the answer

Nobody has published the whole answer for our exact setup: a model-free service, two projects, mixed models. These four are the closest, each for a different part:

Lauren Tan · Cursor

Has the most complete published playbook for a coordinator and many agents: pstack in github.com/cursor/plugins. It covers how to brief, isolate and verify, and how to build trust before adding more agents. Follow its mechanics, but drop its pre-merge verdict, as she does on her own projects.

Ryan Lopopolo · OpenAI

The closest to your goal of land fast and review after merge. "Corrections are cheap, and waiting is expensive." Short instructions, a fast build, and cleanup agents running after merge.

Wilson Lin · Cursor research

Has measured evidence that shared locks and a merging bottleneck kill throughput, and that planners plus isolated workers scale.

Steve Yegge · Gas Town and Beads

The architecture closest to Herdr, and he names Herdr. His overfencing collapse and the cut from 650 refusals to 14 are our story; his merge-agent and batch-landing lessons apply directly.

If you agree: the build order

This replaces the line-service patching. Each phase ends with a measured check: hours with ready work and no engineer, and minutes from build to main.

Phase 0 · config
  • Lift the superseded hold.
  • Reconcile, then remove, the leftover review folders.
  • Set Brain's review selection policy and add the design route on BCH.
  • Drop the report-first flag from briefs.
  • Set the queue to your priorities.
  • Cut instructions to one page per role.
  • Apply the permission rule set.
Phase 1 · line
  • Start workers in parallel up to capacity, without the main loop waiting on them.
  • A blocked report holds the item.
  • Steps that are safe to repeat are retried.
  • A review is owed for every landed commit.
  • The memory wait follows the 10-18 rule.
  • Ingest as soon as the report exists.
  • One cleanup owner.
Phase 2 · after merge
  • Main health: full suite, find the breaking change, revert it with a new commit and file a fix item to its owner.
  • Reviews on a different model; findings become items.
  • Flaky-test quarantine.
  • Weekly spot-check of the reviewer.
Phase 3 · split
  • A planner role splits items into children with declared areas.
  • Parallel lanes, plus ordered chains for overlapping areas.
  • A whole-feature check on main.
BCH now
  • Publication preparation reports every refusal at once.
  • Approvals become record entries, not code.
  • Line caps become advice.
  • Rehearsal and withdrawal engineering continue throughout; none of this holds them.
