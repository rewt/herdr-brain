# Count failures, fence scope, file every discovery

Autonomous Codex and Claude sessions stay aligned to a North Star when the line, not the agent's conscientiousness, bounds the work. Five structures do the bounding: a checkable done predicate written before work starts; a count of failed attempts that forces a new frame after two or three; one owner who decides scope and never fixes; a written fence naming what must not change; and a "file it, don't do it" channel for everything discovered along the way. The published numbers cluster tightly. SWE-agent's odds of eventually succeeding at an edit fall from 90.5% to 57.2% after a single failed edit. LLM debugging loses 60-80% of its effectiveness within two or three attempts. The stop rules of Superpowers, pstack and the Claude Code team all fire at two or three failures. Vendors now name the opposite failure too. Anthropic says explicit verification instructions cause over-verification on its current models, and that removing them costs nothing in quality. OpenAI's guidance defines "enough evidence" as being able to name the exact change, not as certainty. Tested against the operator's seven failures of 2026-10-26, five were guards with no priced exit: the three-hour stale-lock proof, the eleven one-guard publication runs, the four-way split of a 336-line change, the 32nd file and the UNKNOWN diagnosis. The other two, the day of cascading patches and the backlog route that revived old studies, lacked a circuit breaker or a fence. A cross-family review is worth one pass on judgment-heavy work. But the only controlled Claude-versus-Codex study found the effect asymmetric: +18.1 points when Claude reviewed Codex, −8.6 the other way. It used older models on competitive-programming tasks, so the operator should measure the family split on their own items rather than take it from folklore. Almost every threshold in this report is practitioner judgment, and no controlled study covers multi-agent troubleshooting of real systems.

## Converging agents stop after two or three failed attempts

### Every repair system that converges puts a number on its loop

The automated repair systems that reliably finish are rigid. Agentless never lets the model choose an action. It runs three fixed phases: localization, repair and patch validation. Localization narrows from files to functions to lines. Repair produces 40 candidate patches per issue. Validation picks one using generated reproduction tests, the existing regression suite and a majority vote. It solved 32% of SWE-bench Lite at about $0.70 per issue ([Agentless](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01489v2)). AutoCodeRover stops when "a patch is generated or the context retrieval stage repeats ten times." Its patch stage gets three tries before it returns the best patch so far ([AutoCodeRover](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05427v3)). SWE-agent gives each instance a $4 budget and submits whatever edits exist when that runs out. It also caps the interface: a 100-line file viewer, at most 50 search results, and a lint check that reverts edits introducing syntax errors ([SWE-agent](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15793)). OpenHands ships a stuck detector that is on by default. It halts when the same action produces the same observation four times, or the same error three times, or when two actions alternate for six cycles ([OpenHands docs](https://docs.openhands.dev/sdk/guides/agent-stuck-detector)). That detector also shows the trap in any counter. Its hardcoded thresholds killed a legitimate job that polled every five minutes. The fix, closed in December 2025, made the thresholds configurable so intended waits could be exempted ([OpenHands issue #762](https://github.com/OpenHands/software-agent-sdk/issues/762)).

The trajectory data explains why the caps work. **Resolved SWE-agent runs finished at a median of 12 steps and $1.21. Unresolved runs averaged 21 steps and $2.52.** 93% of resolved instances finished before their budget ran out. **An edit attempt had a 90.5% chance of eventually succeeding, which fell to 57.2% after one failed edit**, and cascading failed edits made up 23.4% of failures ([SWE-agent](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15793)). The Debugging Decay Index finds that "most models lose 60-80% of their debugging capability within just 2-3 attempts," and proposes a "strategic fresh start" at the point of decay ([Debugging Decay Index](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18403)). Self-repair research adds a further finding: once its cost is counted, a model critiquing its own code gains little, while feedback from a stronger model gains substantially more ([Olausson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896)). Together these say a run that has gone past the typical resolved length, or has failed the same thing twice, is statistically in the losing population. The rational move is to change who is thinking or what they believe, not to try harder. None of these systems contains a "keep verifying until certain" step. Verification is one external oracle applied once.

### Practitioners fire their stop rules at the same count

The individual practitioners arrive at the same threshold by different routes. Jesse Vincent's Superpowers debugging skill counts failed fixes. Under three, the agent returns to root-cause investigation. At three or more, it must "STOP and question the architecture." It does not attempt a fourth fix without discussing it with the human, because **"This is NOT a failed hypothesis - this is a wrong architecture."** The skill names a second trigger as well: each fix "exposes new coupling or symptoms elsewhere" ([systematic-debugging SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/systematic-debugging/SKILL.md)). Its review-fix loop caps at five rounds. Rounds four and five go to "a fresh implementer on a more capable model," because "a loop that survives three resumes usually means the implementer cannot see its own problem." Out-of-scope observations "never extend the loop" ([fix-loop.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/subagent-driven-development/references/fix-loop.md)). Lauren Tan's pstack is stricter and more diagnostic: **"When two or more fixes that share one premise have failed the same gate, suspect the premise, not the fixes."** Before the next fix, the agent writes the premise down and finds an observation that would differ if the premise were false ([attack-the-premise](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/principle-attack-the-premise/SKILL.md)). Its bug-fix playbook adds that "Belt-and-suspenders that 'might help' is a hypothesis, not a fix. It does not ship," and that a refuted hypothesis's changes are reverted ([bug-fix.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/bug-fix.md)). The Claude Code documentation gives the human-steering version: "after two failed corrections run `/clear` and write a better prompt instead of a third." A third correction "mostly adds to a conversation that already contains two failed attempts for Claude to keep re-reading" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

The others say the same thing without numbers. Thorsten Ball names the "doom loop," in which the model "tries to fix and fix and fix the same test over and over again," and traces many agent problems to not starting new threads often enough ([How I use Amp](https://ampcode.com/notes/how-i-use-amp)). Geoffrey Huntley's rule is "if it starts going off the rails, then you need to narrow it down to just one item." His remedies are `git reset --hard`, a regenerated plan, and the suspicion that "your specifications may be incorrect" ([Ralph](https://ghuntley.com/ralph/)). Mitchell Hashimoto backs out work he does not understand and sometimes redoes it by hand ([Non-trivial vibing](https://mitchellh.com/writing/non-trivial-vibing)). Armin Ronacher supplies the cautionary data on both sides. His MiniJinja port succeeded with **about 45 minutes of human time, 10 hours of agent time and about $60**, because the goal was mechanical: make every snapshot test pass ([MiniJinja Go port](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/1/14/minijinja-go-port/)). His model-driven "software factory" ran **about 35 hours and cost about $1,200, and produced nothing of value**, because the model, given a slightly too big task, "will continue until it succeeds" ([Astra for Coding](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/9/7/astra-why/)). He also names what unattended iteration does to a system's code: "models tend to observe some local failure and add a local defense," which makes the system "less understandable while appearing more robust" ([The Coming Loop](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/6/23/the-coming-loop/)).

What changes at the stop is the frame, not the patch. The options are a new premise, a fresh context, a stronger or different model, or an architecture question to the owner. A fresh start has a known cost. The Manus team keeps failures in context because "Erasing failure removes evidence" ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)). The two positions reconcile if a reset carries forward the goal, the scope fence and a ledger of failed attempts, and leaves behind only the trajectory. Hard caps belong in the harness, not the prompt. Claude Code exposes `--max-turns` and `--max-budget-usd`, and a `/goal` condition that a separate model checks after every turn. It also overrides a Stop hook that has blocked eight stops in a row ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Every published cap counts attempts, steps or dollars. The research found no wall-clock timebox backed by evidence. That fits Herdr Brain's own contract, which forbids elapsed-only stops and makes budgets advisory (`/Users/rewt/development/rewt/herdr-brain/AGENTS.md`).

### Over-verification is now a named failure, not a virtue

The newest vendor guidance treats extra checking as a defect. Anthropic's Opus 5 prompting guide says: **"Claude Opus 5 verifies its own work without being told to. If your prompt contains explicit verification instructions… remove them: instructions like these cause over-verification on Claude Opus 5, and removing them reduces wasted tokens with no loss in quality. The same applies to legacy harness scaffolding that adds separate verification steps."** The same guide says not to "use subagents to verify or double-check your own work" ([Prompting Claude Opus 5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5)). The Claude Code docs retire "loop until done, don't be lazy" instructions, which "are more likely to cause over-work than fix a stall" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). OpenAI's GPT-5 guide supplies the most usable definition of enough evidence: "Parallelize discovery and stop as soon as you can act." The agent stops when it can "name exact content to change" or when "Top hits converge (~70%) on one area/path." If signals conflict, it escalates once with a single refined batch. Its tightest setting allows "an absolute maximum of 2 tool calls," after which the agent proceeds "even if it might not be fully correct" ([GPT-5 prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt-5/gpt-5_prompting_guide)). In the same guide, Cursor reports that a thoroughness prompt made the model call search "repetitively" on small tasks ([GPT-5 prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt-5/gpt-5_prompting_guide)).

The empirical work agrees. Across 4,018 SWE-bench trajectories, "higher overthinking scores correlate with decreased performance." Analysis paralysis, a shift "from immediate actions to elaborate future planning," is one of three named patterns. Picking the lower-overthinking of two low-effort runs nearly matched high effort, scoring 27.3% against 29.1%, at about 43% lower cost ([Overthinking](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08235)). The MAST taxonomy of more than 1,600 multi-agent traces finds step repetition in 15.7% of failures and agents "unaware of termination conditions" in 12.4%. **Adding one verification step against the high-level task objective raised success by 15.6%** ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)).

The opposite failure is equally documented. Agents mark features complete "without proper testing" and later sessions "declare the job done" early ([Effective harnesses](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). MAST counts premature termination at 6.2% and incomplete verification at 8.2% ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)). The resolution the sources converge on is one external pass/fail check against the item's objective. Its evidence is "the command it ran and its output," and it runs once ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Repeated re-proof of intermediate facts does not count. pstack makes the economics explicit: "A verifier agent whose entire product would be rerunning one command is ceremony, not verification" ([orchestrate.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). Herdr Brain's Verify Once policy already encodes this. One designated party verifies each routine fact, and consumers do not rehash it (`docs/reference/VERIFY-ONCE.md`, referenced from the project's AGENTS.md).

## A North Star bounds work only once it becomes a fence

### Shape Up fixes the budget first and refuses automatic extensions

Basecamp's Shape Up is the most complete human method for keeping work inside a boundary. It fixes an "appetite" before design: "Estimates start with a design and end with a number. Appetites start with a number and end with a design." The reason it gives is that "Without a time limit, there's always a better version" ([Shape Up ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)). Every pitch names its rabbit holes, "Part of a project that is too unknown, complex, or open-ended to bet on," and its no-gos, "functionality or use cases we intentionally aren't covering" ([Shape Up ch. 6](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.5-chapter-06)). Missed work gets no extension: **"If they don't finish, by default the project doesn't get an extension."** That works "like a circuit breaker," and the work returns to shaping ([Shape Up ch. 8](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08)). Backlogs are not inputs to execution. The betting table considers only pitches "from the last six weeks" plus any older pitch someone deliberately revives, because "Backlogs are a big weight we don't need to carry" ([Shape Up ch. 7](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.1-chapter-07)). Work discovered mid-build is recorded with a "~" as a nice-to-have, which "usually… never get[s] built." A loose-task list longer than "three to five items" signals a separate scope ([Shape Up ch. 12](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.3-chapter-12)). Hill charts separate uphill unknowns from downhill execution, and "A dot that doesn't move is effectively a raised hand" ([Shape Up ch. 13](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.4-chapter-13)).

Herdr Brain's contract makes budgets advisory and bans elapsed-only stops, so appetite translates into scope units rather than time: the files an item touches, the approvals it triggers, the items it spawns. The circuit breaker then refuses nothing. An item that exhausts its appetite stops growing and returns a reshape request instead of continuing under the same id. One Shape Up assumption does not transfer. Its "let it go" policy relies on humans forgetting unchosen ideas, and on important ones coming back on their own. Agents forget nothing, so an agent backlog needs explicit expiry or a recurrence count to stand in for "it came back."

### Specs fence a fix by naming what must keep working

Spec-driven tools fix scope before code the same way. They separate what from how, force unknowns into explicit markers, and put a review between phases. AWS Kiro's Bugfix Spec is the best fit for troubleshooting. It has three sections: Current Behavior, Expected Behavior and an Unchanged Behavior section written as "WHEN [condition] THEN the system SHALL CONTINUE TO [existing behavior]". It asks for "Any constraints (code that shouldn't be modified)" and recommends the format for "Situations where previous fix attempts caused regressions" ([Kiro Bugfix Specs](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/bugfix-specs/)). OpenSpec's proposal carries explicit "In scope:" and "Out of scope:" lists, with examples marked "(future work)." Each change lives in a self-contained folder ([OpenSpec concepts](https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec/blob/main/docs/concepts.md)). GitHub Spec Kit makes the agent mark gaps "[NEEDS CLARIFICATION: specific question]" instead of guessing. It forbids "speculative or 'might need' features" and requires any exception to its gates to be justified in writing under "Complexity Tracking" ([spec-driven.md](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/spec-driven.md)). Its "Idea assessment" workflow ends in "An evidence-backed go, clarify, or stop decision" ([Spec Kit README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/spec-kit/main/README.md)). That shape suits research items: their output is a decision, not a prototype.

These tools share a loophole. Spec Kit treats the spec as the source of truth, and OpenSpec lets you "update any artifact anytime" ([OpenSpec README](https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec)). So an agent can widen scope by rewriting the spec in the middle of a fix. For autonomous lines the remedy is procedural: any edit that widens In-scope or removes an Unchanged entry routes to the item's owner. Not everyone plans first. pstack's README says "personally, i don't believe in planning. the best spec is code" ([pstack README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/README.md)). Yet pstack still requires a falsifiable done predicate up front and forbids relaxing it later ([autonomous-run.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/autonomous-run.md)). The disagreement is about prose specs, not about fixing the goal.

### Drift comes from momentum and inherited trajectories, not forgetting

The research on goal drift identifies a cause that matters for agent lines. Arike and colleagues found that "all evaluated models exhibit some degree of goal drift" under environmental pressure. Drift tracks growing "susceptibility to pattern-matching behaviors as the context length grows." Stating the goal strongly reduced drift for every model (p < 0.05), and drift through inaction (failing to undo off-goal work) exceeded drift through action ([Arike et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02709v1)). Menon and colleagues found that models robust to direct pressure "often inherit drift when conditioned on prefilled trajectories from weaker agents." Only GPT-5.1 stayed consistently resilient among the models tested ([Inherited Goal Drift](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03258)). Spreading instructions across turns cut performance by 39% on average, and models that went off track "get lost and do not recover" ([Laban et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)).

The practitioner mitigations are recitation, external state and one task per session. Manus rewrites a `todo.md` to recite its objectives "into the end of the context" ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)). Anthropic's long-running harness gives each session one feature. It keeps a JSON feature list in which agents may only flip a `passes` flag, because "It is unacceptable to remove or edit tests" ([Effective harnesses](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). It also treats "context rot" with compaction and with notes kept outside the context ([Effective context engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). None of these mitigations has controlled evidence in coding agents, and no benchmark measures scope expansion (spawned items, growing file sets) as drift. Two implications still follow. A goal check should ask "is the original problem resolved yet?", because drift by inaction is real. And a session handed an old study's trajectory inherits that trajectory's momentum.

### Incident command separates the scope owner from the fixer

Incident disciplines bound troubleshooting with roles, not willpower. In Google's SRE model, the Incident Commander holds the high-level state. "the operations team should be the only group modifying the system during an incident," and a Planning role handles "filing bugs" for later. In Google's example of an unmanaged incident, an engineer freelancing changes made "a bad situation far worse." The first priority is to "stop the bleeding, restore service, and preserve the evidence for root-causing" ([SRE book ch. 14](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/)). Google mitigates before it finds the root cause: "you only need to know the location of the root cause" to apply a mitigation ([SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/incident-response/)).

PagerDuty's rules are sharper still. **"Delegate all repair actions, the Incident Commander is NOT a resolver."** Subject-matter experts "Announce all suggestions for resolution to the Incident Commander" and take no action until told. The Scribe records "`TODO` notes… that indicate follow-ups slated for later" ([PagerDuty: During an Incident](https://response.pagerduty.com/during/during_an_incident/)). Each task goes to a named individual with a timebox ("I'll come back to you in X minutes"). The training adds two maxims: "Making the 'wrong' decision is better than making no decision," and, for tangents, **"We can either get you that list, or fix the incident. Not both"** ([PagerDuty IC training](https://response.pagerduty.com/training/incident_commander/)). The Incident Command System restates objectives each operational period and treats one supervisor to five subordinates as the optimal span of control ([FEMA NIMS](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/2020-07/fema_nims_doctrine-2017.pdf)). The XP spike is the research-side counterpart: build only what answers the question, and "Most spikes are not good enough to keep, so expect to throw it away" ([XP spike](http://www.extremeprogramming.org/rules/spike.html)). In agent terms, the troubleshooting worker gets a "file follow-up" verb and no mandate to act on what it files. A study's output is knowledge, and any prototype is new scope.

### A North Star must be decomposed before it can say no

A North Star filters work only when it is concrete enough to test a task against. Amplitude's framing is deliberately indirect: "If you can move your North Star directly, it's probably not a good North Star." The metric is driven by three to five inputs that teams "directly influence with their day-to-day work" ([Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/blog/good-bad-north-star-metric)). Google's OKR guide recommends "just three to five objectives" with about three measurable key results each. Because OKRs are public, declining a request becomes a response to a commitment rather than a personal dispute ([re:Work](https://rework.withgoogle.com/en/guides/set-goals-with-okrs)). A North Star stated bare in a prompt is too abstract for an agent to apply. It has to arrive as a per-item objective and predicate. Parking lots capture off-scope items visibly, with a who, what and when for each, and without follow-up they "quickly become a graveyard" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parking-lots/)). For agents the failure runs the other way. They fill the lot at volume and then treat it as a work queue.

## Two model families help only when the problem is partitioned

### Hierarchy with one writer beats peers

Every source that reports multi-agent success uses a hierarchy with one writer per surface. Anthropic's research system, a lead agent with parallel subagents, beat a single agent by 90.2% on an internal research eval. Its early versions spawned "50 subagents for simple queries" and duplicated work. The fix was a brief stating "an objective, an output format, guidance on the tools and sources to use, and clear task boundaries," plus effort tiers: "Simple fact-finding requires just 1 agent with 3-10 tool calls" ([Anthropic research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Cognition's 2025 warning was that "Actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results" ([Don't Build Multi-Agents](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents)). In April 2026 it said the warning "still hold[s] today for parallel-writer swarms." What works is a narrower set: generator-verifier review, a "smart friend" consult, a manager over children, and read-only search ([Multi-Agents: What's Actually Working](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)). At Cursor, peer agents claiming tasks through a locked file slowed so badly that "Twenty agents would slow down to the effective throughput of two or three." Planners, workers and a per-cycle judge worked, though "periodic fresh starts" were still needed against drift ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). The largest controlled study covered 260 configurations. Against a single agent, multi-agent results ranged **"from +80.8% on decomposable financial reasoning to -70.0% on sequential planning,"** and architectures without centralized verification propagated more errors ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)).

### Parallel troubleshooting pays off only after something partitions the problem

Troubleshooting is often sequential, which is the case where extra agents hurt. Nicholas Carlini's sixteen parallel Claudes all hit the same Linux kernel bug and overwrote one another's fixes: "Having 16 agents running didn't help because each was stuck solving the same task." The fix partitioned the work rather than adding coordination. GCC became a known-good oracle, so each agent compiled a random subset of files with the new compiler, and delta debugging isolated file pairs that failed only together ([Anthropic C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). Claude Code's agent-teams docs recommend parallel teammates for "Debugging with competing hypotheses," because "a single agent tends to find one plausible explanation and stop looking." Teammates try to disprove each other's theories, and the lead writes the consensus to a findings document. The same docs say sequential work suits a single session better ([Agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)).

Debate itself converges only under structure. "Majority Voting alone accounts for most of the performance gains typically attributed to MAD" (multi-agent debate) ([Debate or Vote](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17536)). Models "shift from correct to incorrect answers in response to peer reasoning," favoring agreement over challenge ([Talk Isn't Always Cheap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05396)). The protocol the evidence supports has four steps. Collect independent first answers before agents see each other. Run one critique round. Let a single owner decide on executable evidence. Escalate any disagreement that survives the cap. Review loops need the same cap. "A reviewer that's asked to find problems will always find *something*. If you fix every finding, you end up with extra abstraction layers and tests for cases that can't happen" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). OpenAI warns that its Codex-in-Claude review gate "can create a long-running Claude/Codex loop and may drain usage limits quickly" ([codex-plugin-cc](https://github.com/openai/codex-plugin-cc)). Every's plugin discards "a finding that merely prefers a different approach" when the plan records the decision as settled ([ce-code-review](https://github.com/EveryInc/compound-engineering-plugin/blob/main/docs/guides/ce-code-review.md)).

### Cross-family review is asymmetric and thinly measured

The case for a different-family reviewer rests on mechanisms. LLM judges recognize their own generations and prefer them ([Panickssery et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). Models show a 64.5% blind spot for errors attributed to themselves ([Self-Correction Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02778)). A fresh-session review beat a same-session second look, with an F1 of 28.6% against 21.7% ([Cross-Context Review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12123)). The only controlled Claude-versus-Codex study gives this result ([Xiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21656)):

| Writer | Reviewer | Pass rate | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Codex (GPT-5.5) | none | 71.6% | |
| Codex | Claude (Opus 4.7) | 89.7% | +18.1 points |
| Codex | Codex | 84.5% | +12.9 points |
| Claude | none | 91.4% | |
| Claude | Codex | 82.8% | −8.6 points |
| Claude | Claude | 91.4% | unchanged |

Claude reviewing Codex fixed 26 tasks and broke 5. Codex reviewing Claude fixed 3 and broke 13. The two cross-model orderings were not statistically separable from each other. The study used one model pair, 116 competitive-programming tasks, a static review with no test access, and a baseline capability gap between the two writers ([Xiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21656v1)). The direction of the effect follows capability. A stronger reviewer lifts a weaker author, and a weaker or over-eager reviewer regresses correct work. The counter-evidence is serious too. **Across more than 350 LLMs, "models agree 60% of the time when both models err," and stronger models have more correlated errors "even with distinct architectures and providers"** ([Correlated Errors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962)). Aggregating outputs from only the single best model beat mixing models by 6.6% on AlpacaEval 2.0 ([Rethinking Mixture-of-Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00674)). Cognition gets useful review from the same model in a clean context: "roughly 58%" of the average two bugs it catches per PR are severe ([Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)).

Practice has moved toward cross-family review anyway. pstack runs "a unit's verifier on a different model family from its worker," but only for units "whose verification is expensive, judgment-laden, or high-blast-radius" ([orchestrate.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). Its interrogate skill holds that "The adversarial signal comes from model diversity, not assigned personas," and does "NOT auto-apply changes" ([interrogate](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/interrogate/SKILL.md)). Every's plugin routes its adversarial lens to a different provider "in a separate read-only process" ([ce-code-review](https://github.com/EveryInc/compound-engineering-plugin/blob/main/docs/guides/ce-code-review.md)). Amp's oracle is a read-only OpenAI model, GPT-5.4 as of March 2026, that sits beside an Anthropic main agent. It is invoked explicitly for review, debugging and "figuring out what to do next" ([Amp oracle](https://ampcode.com/news/oracle); [GPT-5.4 oracle](https://ampcode.com/news/gpt-5.4-the-new-oracle)). Because errors correlate even across vendors, a second family does not replace executable checks. Tests remain the only non-circular reference ([Zietsman](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25773)).

### Practitioners split the families differently, so the split must be measured

The attributed reports disagree on which family should do what. Cursor found GPT-5.2 "performed much better on extended autonomous work," while Opus 4.5 "tends to stop earlier and take shortcuts when convenient" ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). Ronacher had Claude Opus 4.5 build the MiniJinja port's structure and GPT-5.2 Codex take "the long tail of test fixing" ([MiniJinja Go port](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/1/14/minijinja-go-port/)). He later found that Codex models did not show an edit-tool regression that hit Opus 4.8 in about 20% of calls in one session ([Better Models: Worse Tools](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/7/4/better-models-worse-tools/)). Thorsten Ball now uses GPT-6 Astra most, and Claude Fable for UI and design ([Thorsten's profile](https://ampcode.com/docs/using-amp/thorsten)). One XDA writer gives Claude Code the UI and Codex "the patient, unglamorous work of figuring out what's actually broken," and has each review the other ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/use-claude-code-and-codex-together-combination-does-something-neither-can-do-alone/)). Peter Steinberger drives with Codex and sends specs to GPT-5-Pro for review ([Just Talk To It](https://steipete.me/posts/just-talk-to-it)). pstack sends judgment and the hardest changes to Opus 5.5 ([pstack README](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/README.md)). Superpowers ignores vendor and tiers by capability. Its rule is to "use a model at least one tier above the implementer that got stuck" ([subagent-driven-development](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/subagent-driven-development/SKILL.md)). Steve Yegge reports a model-specific failure to converge: Opus 4.7's "'just two more things' tic… prevented Opus from ever converging" ([The Shape of Things to Come](https://yegge.ai/essays/the-shape-of-things-to-come/)). Anthropic's own guide warns that Opus 5 "can also expand the scope of a task" ([Prompting Claude Opus 5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5)).

Three rules survive the disagreement. The reviewer should be at least as capable as the author in the domain. It should differ in family, or at minimum in context. And the operator should measure the split by logging which cross-family findings proved real, because brand folklore is already out of date. Most of the reports above concern models two or three generations old.

### The over-guarding failures turned cheap reversible decisions into long round trips

The stale-lock episode, failure (1), is the clearest case of documented over-verification. The hypothesis "the lock is stale because its owner process is dead" needs one liveness check and one check that the PID still belongs to the original holder. Superpowers' method is to write that hypothesis down, run the single-variable test, and move to the fix ([systematic-debugging](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/systematic-debugging/SKILL.md)). Nothing in it licenses re-proving a confirmed hypothesis. OpenAI's risk ratings would put removing a local, recoverable lock at medium at most ([OpenAI practical guide](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)). pstack's coordinator store does not involve an agent at all: its CLI replaces a stale lock when the holder PID is dead ([orch.ts](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/scripts/orch/orch.ts)). Two supervisors proving the same fact also broke Herdr Brain's own Verify Once rule, and the multi-agent evidence predicts no gain from two agents on a sequential check ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)). The guard did exist for a reason. Yegge's Deacon was "cleaning up 'stale' workers that weren't stale and murdering entire crews mid-task" ([Gas Town Emergency User Manual](https://yegge.ai/essays/gas-town-emergency-user-manual/)). The lesson from both stories is to make the reclaim mechanical rather than deliberative. The check tests PID and identity, never transcript or file modification time, because "Transcript mtime is not liveness" ([orchestrate.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). The lock's contents are archived before removal, which preserves the evidence ([SRE book](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/)). With the check mechanical, three hours of judgment drop to a few seconds of code.

The eleven-run publication push, failure (2), was serial discovery multiplied by escalation latency. Ten gaps of 18 to 64 minutes come to roughly three to eleven hours of waiting before any run time is counted. Every published mechanism points the other way. OpenAI's guide says to "Parallelize discovery and stop as soon as you can act" ([GPT-5 guide](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt-5/gpt-5_prompting_guide)). Agentless runs the whole regression suite once to see every failure before it chooses a patch ([Agentless](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01489v2)). Ronacher says "Tools must clearly inform agents of misuse or errors to ensure forward progress" ([Agentic Coding Recommendations](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2025/6/12/agentic-coding/)). OpenAI's harness team writes lint error messages that carry the fix ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex)). pstack keeps "retries, CI flake triage… and 'should I keep going'" away from the human, and batches real escalations into the status page ([orchestrate.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). The guards on an outward publication are legitimate. Superpowers names "a publish" among its four stop reasons ([subagent-driven-development](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/subagent-driven-development/SKILL.md)). The defect was that the guards revealed themselves one per run. A dry-run preflight that evaluates every guard and returns the full list would have turned eleven round trips into one. Yegge's experience shows where unowned guards lead: Wheelhouse grew "650 distinct refusal sites across 173 scripts" until "no work was legal," and recovered after cutting them to 14 fences with a single curator ([Seats and Sunsets](https://yegge.ai/essays/seats-and-sunsets/)).

The 300-line cap, failure (3), and the 32nd file, failure (6), are both fences that worked as refusals. Small changes are well supported. Google calls 100 lines "usually a reasonable size" and 1,000 lines "usually too large," and requires each change in a stack to keep the system working ([Google small CLs](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)). But no source treats size as a hard stop. Spec Kit's gates allow an exception justified in writing ([spec-driven.md](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/spec-driven.md)). Herdr Brain's own contract says budgets are advisory: "surface them, never refuse work because of them" (`/Users/rewt/development/rewt/herdr-brain/AGENTS.md`). A change 12% over the cap became four runs, each with its own dispositions, landing and review, so the cap quadrupled the coordination it was meant to reduce. In failure (6), the approved 31-file set should have been the Unchanged fence. pstack's answers apply directly. "Put out-of-band fixes in their own PR" ([autonomous-run.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/autonomous-run.md)). And "a defect trunk already has is recorded against trunk and does not gate the PR" ([autopilot-full.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/autopilot-full.md)). The timing rule's complaint belonged in its own change on main, with the publication left untouched. The one exception follows Shape Up's must-have test ([Shape Up ch. 14](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.5-chapter-14)). If the defect changes what gets published, it is a must-have, and the re-approval is the correct price.

The UNKNOWN diagnosis, failure (7), shows two evidence registers being confused. For verification verdicts, refusing to guess is right. pstack holds that "Inconclusive is not a pass" ([figure-it-out](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/figure-it-out/SKILL.md)), and Herdr Brain says "Unrun checks are never PASS." Superpowers likewise flags "should," "probably" and "seems to" in completion claims ([verification-before-completion](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/verification-before-completion/SKILL.md)). Applied to diagnosis, the same rule produces paralysis. A diagnosis is a ranked bet. Anthropic's research prompt asks for "several competing hypotheses" with tracked "confidence levels" ([Claude prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Google mitigates once it knows only "the location of the root cause" ([SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/incident-response/)). Superpowers records "Ruling: <what you decided> — <why> — <what it costs if wrong>," because "a session parked on a question costs their whole day and buys nothing" ([subagent-driven-development](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/subagent-driven-development/SKILL.md)). Herdr Brain's doctrine of "unknown held" and "a missing address stays UNKNOWN" is correct for routing and verdicts. Without a separate diagnosis format, that evidence rule reads as if it also governs cause analysis, which is this report's best explanation for the UNKNOWN.

### The under-fencing failures had no breaker and no boundary

The day of line-service patches, failure (4), hit Superpowers' second stop trigger exactly: each fix "exposes new coupling or symptoms elsewhere," which signals a wrong architecture rather than a bad hypothesis ([systematic-debugging](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/systematic-debugging/SKILL.md)). Ronacher's "local defense" describes what the patches were doing to the line service ([The Coming Loop](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/6/23/the-coming-loop/)). The prescribed response is not a redesign. pstack's step is cheap: write the premise down and test it before the next fix ([attack-the-premise](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/principle-attack-the-premise/SKILL.md)). If the deciding fact took a deep dig to find, the agent should "propose the cheap, always-on signal that would have shown it at first look" ([fix-root-causes](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/principle-fix-root-causes/SKILL.md)). If the premise is refuted, Shape Up's circuit breaker applies. The item does not get extended; it returns to shaping as a separate architecture item that its owner selects ([Shape Up ch. 8](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08)).

The backlog route, failure (5), reversed Shape Up's most explicit rule. Old ideas reach execution only when someone deliberately revives one, and personal lists are never "direct inputs to the betting process" ([Shape Up ch. 7](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.1-chapter-07)). The studies were spikes, whose output is knowledge and whose code is thrown away ([XP spike](http://www.extremeprogramming.org/rules/spike.html)). Resuming them handed a fresh session their trajectory, and with it the inherited momentum that Menon and colleagues measured ([Inherited Goal Drift](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03258)). Yegge reports the same pattern in Wheelhouse, which "spammed the players with features" until he added gates ([Seats and Sunsets](https://yegge.ai/essays/seats-and-sunsets/)). A route should take an explicit list of item ids or a freshness window, the agent analogue of Shape Up's six weeks. It should treat a study's output as a decision, and any item a study spawns should be a proposal waiting for selection.

## A bounded troubleshooting method Herdr Brain can adopt

The method below assembles the cited mechanisms into one loop for Codex and Claude sessions working a problem together. The supervisor of an item acts as incident commander. It writes the item contract and owns scope, and it never edits code. One worker, in one family, is the only writer on the item's surface. That worker runs one broad discovery batch, records hypotheses in a register on the item, tests the leading hypothesis with a single-variable check, and acts once it can name the exact change. A failed fix is counted against its premise. Two failures on one premise force the premise to be written down and tested. Three failures, or any fix that exposes a new issue, end the item's patching. The reframe then goes to a fresh session in the other family, which carries the contract and the failure ledger but not the trajectory. If that session's first frame also fails, the item returns to its owner for reshaping. Everything discovered along the way is filed as a record, and nothing filed is done. Every rule is a count or a fence, never an elapsed timer. That keeps the method inside Herdr Brain's contract: advisory budgets, no stops on elapsed time alone, and refusal only of what would make the record inconsistent.

The assignment rule deliberately avoids a fixed brand split. The evidence cannot support one: Cursor, Ronacher, Ball, Amp and XDA each assign the families differently, and the controlled study's direction depends on which writer is weaker. The supportable choices are structural. The family that wrote a change does not review it. The reviewer is at least as strong as the author. Cross-family review is reserved for units where judgment or blast radius justifies it, matching pstack's warning that a verifier that only reruns a command is "ceremony." After two weeks, the log of real and dismissed cross-family findings, and of which family trips the stop rule more often per item type, becomes the first evidence anyone has on this split for repository-scale troubleshooting.

## Seven disagreements, and the evidence nobody has

### Where the sources disagree

The first disagreement is whom to escalate to. Superpowers' debugging skill stops at three failed fixes and talks to the human ([systematic-debugging](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/systematic-debugging/SKILL.md)). Its plan-execution skill instead records a ruling and keeps going ([subagent-driven-development](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obra/superpowers/main/skills/subagent-driven-development/SKILL.md)). pstack keeps "should I keep going" away from the human entirely ([orchestrate.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/orchestrate.md)). For Herdr Brain, the reconciliation is to escalate to the item's supervisor and reserve the operator for reserved decisions. The second disagreement is whether a plateau means stop. pstack says "A plateau is not a stop… pivot your approach" ([autonomous-run.md](https://github.com/cursor/plugins/blob/main/pstack/skills/poteto-mode/playbooks/autonomous-run.md)), while Shape Up's circuit breaker and Superpowers' cap say stop. They agree if a pivot means a new premise: continuing on the same premise is what both forbid.

The third disagreement is over planning. pstack's author says "the best spec is code," while Spec Kit, Kiro, Hashimoto and Boris Cherny ("Most sessions start in Plan mode") put a plan first ([pstack README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cursor/plugins/main/pstack/README.md); [Cherny thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/2007179832300581177.html)). Both camps fix a checkable goal before work, so the disagreement is about prose, not boundaries. The fourth is whether to use multiple agents at all. Cognition and Huntley argue for a single thread ([Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents); [Ralph](https://ghuntley.com/ralph/)). Anthropic reports a 90.2% gain on research, and its agent-teams docs recommend parallel competing hypotheses. Kim et al.'s range from +80.8% to −70% says the answer depends on whether the task decomposes.

The fifth disagreement is whether cross-family review is worth it. pstack, Every and Amp build it in. Cognition gets severe-bug catches from the same model in a clean context. Correlated-error and mixture-of-agents studies show diversity's ceiling, and the one direct test is asymmetric. The sixth is which family does what, where the practitioner reports point in opposite directions and mostly concern superseded models. The seventh is what to do with discoveries. NN/g says capture and follow up every parked item. Shape Up says let unchosen ideas go. Agents force a middle rule: capture everything, dispatch nothing without selection, and expire what never recurs.

### What nobody has measured

The evidence base has five holes that matter for this method. No controlled study measures multi-agent or cross-model troubleshooting on real systems. The agent-teams "competing hypotheses" pattern is vendor guidance without numbers. The 2-versus-3 thresholds come from practitioner judgment. The Debugging Decay Index measures single-function code generation, and SWE-agent's decay figure measures edits, not diagnoses. No published study measures serial one-blocker-per-run discovery, or the value of enumerating every guard first. The preflight recommendation is inference from adjacent mechanisms. The only Claude-versus-Codex review study uses Opus 4.7 and GPT-5.5 on static competitive-programming review. Current models such as Opus 5.5 and the GPT-6 family are untested, and no study covers repository-scale review with test access. Goal-drift benchmarks test value conflicts such as profit against emissions, not task expansion, so nothing measures whether recitation, fences or fresh contexts reduce spawned items or growing file sets.

The sourcing has limits too. OpenAI's harness-engineering essay could not be fetched, and Lauren Tan's talk content is known only from secondhand write-ups. Every throughput figure from practitioners is self-reported.

## Conclusion

The operator's day reads at first as agents being too eager in some places and too timid in others. The research suggests a single defect underneath: rules in the line that have no priced exit. A guard with no exception path turns a reversible two-minute decision into an 18-to-64-minute round trip. A cap with no justification field turns 36 extra lines into three extra runs. A route with no fence turns a dormant backlog into live work. A diagnosis format with no confidence field turns an obvious cause into UNKNOWN. Most of the fixes therefore remove scaffolding rather than add it. They move numbers into the harness, which counts attempts, reclaims dead locks and enumerates guards. They move judgment to one owner per item. And they make "file it" the default verb for discovery. That is also why the method fits Herdr Brain's existing contract, which already says budgets are advisory, status is derived and one party verifies each fact. Several of the failures came from local rules that drifted away from that contract, not from the contract itself.

The cross-model question turns out to be less about which family is smarter than about decorrelation at the moments where a single agent fails: the stuck point, the review of a judgment call, and the competing-hypotheses fork. A second family is most valuable as a fresh frame after the stop rule fires, which is exactly where SWE-agent's and the Debugging Decay Index's numbers show the first agent's odds collapsing. Because the published family splits disagree and describe models already superseded, the operator's own ledger of stop-rule trips and real cross-family findings is the only evidence that will fit Herdr Brain's projects.
