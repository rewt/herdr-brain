# North Star alignment and scope control for autonomous troubleshooting

All links below were fetched on 2026-10-26 unless a Gaps entry says otherwise. Quoted strings come from the fetched pages, extracted through a fetch tool. Treat them as near-verbatim and check the page before quoting them in a publication.

## 1. Basecamp Shape Up: appetite, rabbit holes and no-gos, the circuit breaker, hill charts. How do they map onto agent work items?

### Takeaway
Shape Up controls scope with four rules. Time is fixed before any design ("appetite"). Known traps and excluded cases are written into the pitch ("rabbit holes" and "no-gos"). Work that misses its time box gets no automatic extension ("circuit breaker"). Status separates unsolved unknowns from plain execution ("hill chart"). It also rejects backlogs as an input to execution: old ideas reach the betting table only when someone deliberately brings them back. That rule speaks directly to the "backlog route ran old studies" failure.

### Cited Findings
**Appetite: fixed time, variable scope (Ch. 3, Set Boundaries)**
- An appetite is "a time budget for a standard team size". It is set before solutions are discussed, and it is a limit, not a forecast. — [Shape Up Ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)
- "Estimates start with a design and end with a number. Appetites start with a number and end with a design." — [Shape Up Ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)
- "Without a time limit, there's always a better version." "The best is relative to your constraints." A fixed time box "pushes them to make decisions about what is core". — [Shape Up Ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)
- There are two sizes. A small batch is one designer plus one or two programmers for one to two weeks. A big batch is the same team for the full six weeks. Work too big for six weeks is narrowed, or a meaningful part is split off and shaped separately. — [Shape Up Ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)
- The default response to a raw idea is "Interesting. Maybe some day." It is a soft no that keeps options open. A yes given too early creates commitments the team does not yet understand. — [Shape Up Ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)
- Grab-bag projects (for example "redesign the Files section", or anything labelled "2.0") have no clear start or end. Shape Up reframes them around one problem by asking "What's really going wrong?" In one case a request for complex permission rules became a warning on the archive action: a one-day change instead of a six-week project. — [Shape Up Ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)

**Rabbit holes and out-of-bounds (Ch. 5, Risks and Rabbit Holes; Ch. 6, Write the Pitch)**
- "all it takes is one hole in the concept to derail that." The method is to walk a use case "in slow motion" and ask whether each part needs new technical work, assumes how parts fit together, or assumes an undesigned solution exists. — [Shape Up Ch. 5](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.4-chapter-05)
- Holes are patched in the shaped solution rather than left to the builders: "It's not responsible to give the team a tangled knot of interdependencies". — [Shape Up Ch. 5](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.4-chapter-05)
- Declaring out of bounds means to "call out any cases you specifically aren't supporting to keep the project well within the appetite". Cutting back starts from the rule that "everyone should start from the assumption that the feature is valuable without it." — [Shape Up Ch. 5](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.4-chapter-05)
- When shapers consult technical experts, they ask "is X possible in 6-weeks?" rather than "Is this possible?", because "we're really hunting for time bombs that might blow up the project once it's committed to a team." — [Shape Up Ch. 5](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.4-chapter-05)
- A pitch has five ingredients: Problem, Appetite, Solution, Rabbit holes, No-gos. A rabbit hole is "Part of a project that is too unknown, complex, or open-ended to bet on". No-gos are "functionality or use cases we intentionally aren't covering". The worked examples are "no custom domains in v1" (a rabbit hole closed off) and "no WYSIWYG editing" (a no-go). — [Shape Up Ch. 6](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.5-chapter-06)

**Circuit breaker and bugs (Ch. 8, The Betting Table)**
- "If they don't finish, by default the project doesn't get an extension." It works "like a circuit breaker that ensures one project doesn't overload the system." A miss goes back to shaping in the next cycle instead of receiving more time. "the most we can lose is six weeks." — [Shape Up Ch. 8](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08)
- On bugs: "There is nothing special about bugs that makes them automatically more important than everything else." "The vast majority of bugs can wait six weeks or longer, and many don't even need to be fixed." Only real crises justify interrupting work, and "crises are rare." — [Shape Up Ch. 8](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08)
- A bug too large for cool-down is shaped as a pitch, so that "instead of interrupting other work, the people at the betting table can make a deliberate decision." — [Shape Up Ch. 8](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08)
- A two-week cool-down follows each cycle with "no scheduled work". During it, project teams are "free to work on whatever they want." — [Shape Up Ch. 8](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08)

**Bets, not backlogs (Ch. 7)**
- "Backlogs are a big weight we don't need to carry." "The growing pile gives us a feeling like we're always behind even though we're not." — [Shape Up Ch. 7](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.1-chapter-07)
- The betting table looks only at pitches "from the last six weeks", plus any older pitch someone deliberately revives. "Nothing else is on the table." — [Shape Up Ch. 7](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.1-chapter-07)
- People keep their own lists, but "none of these lists are direct inputs to the betting process." "Really important ideas will come back to you." Ideas that are not chosen: "we let it go." "There's nothing we need to track or hold on to." — [Shape Up Ch. 7](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.1-chapter-07)

**Work discovered while building (Ch. 12, Map the Scopes; Ch. 14, Decide When to Stop)**
- The book separates imagined tasks ("Work the teams decide they need to do after just thinking about the project") from discovered tasks ("Tasks the team discovers they need to do after they start getting involved in the real work"). It also says "Scope mapping isn't planning. You need to walk the territory before you can draw the map." — [Shape Up Ch. 12](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.3-chapter-12)
- The "Chowder" list holds loose tasks: "If it gets longer than three to five items, something is fishy and there's probably a scope to be drawn somewhere." — [Shape Up Ch. 12](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.3-chapter-12)
- New improvements found mid-work are recorded "as tasks on the scope but mark them with a ~ in front". These are nice-to-haves, built only if time remains, and "usually they never get built." Issues QA finds default to nice-to-haves, and the team promotes one to must-have only by severity. — [Shape Up Ch. 12](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.3-chapter-12); [Shape Up Ch. 14](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.5-chapter-14)
- "Instead of comparing up against the ideal, compare down to baseline." "Scope grows naturally", so teams need the authority to keep cutting. The book calls this "scope hammering". Its questions are: is this a must-have, could we ship without it, what happens if we skip it, how many customers does it affect. — [Shape Up Ch. 14](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.5-chapter-14)
- Extensions are "very rare". Two conditions must both hold: what remains are true must-haves that survived hammering, and all remaining work is downhill, with no open questions. — [Shape Up Ch. 14](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.5-chapter-14)

**Hill charts (Ch. 13, Show Progress)**
- "The uphill phase is full of uncertainty, unknowns, and problem solving. The downhill phase is marked by certainty, confidence, seeing everything, and knowing what to do." — [Shape Up Ch. 13](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.4-chapter-13)
- "A dot that doesn't move is effectively a raised hand: 'Something might be wrong here.'" Teams otherwise "hide uncertainty and accumulate risk". — [Shape Up Ch. 13](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.4-chapter-13)
- Teams take the scopes with the most unknowns first. Backsliding happens when "somebody did the uphill work with their head instead of their hands". The three thirds of the uphill are "I've thought about this", "I've validated my approach", and "I don't believe there are other unknowns". — [Shape Up Ch. 13](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.4-chapter-13)

### Inferences
- **Appetite as a field on each work item.** Every item gets a declared budget before work starts. In this operator's setting, the project contract makes budgets advisory and forbids elapsed-only stops. So appetite is better expressed in scope units than in time: files or artifacts touched, new items spawned, approvals triggered. In the 31-to-32-file case, the appetite would be "stay inside the 31-file approved set". Anything beyond it is a no-go that gets filed, not done.
- **No-gos and rabbit holes as explicit item fields.** Write "Not in this item:" and "Known traps and their patch:" into the dispatch brief. Any action that touches a listed no-go becomes a filed item, not a change. This moves the scope decision from the agent mid-troubleshoot back to whoever shaped the item.
- **Circuit breaker, translated.** No silent extension. When an item exhausts its appetite or finds that the fix needs a no-go, it stops expanding and returns a reshape request. It does not continue under the same item. That fits an advisory-budget rule because nothing is refused: the item is closed and re-shaped rather than grown.
- **Bets, not backlogs, as the direct counter to the backlog-route example.** In Shape Up a backlog is never a dispatchable input. Only a deliberate bet from recent pitches, or an explicitly revived one, reaches execution. A route that automatically feeds old research studies into execution reverses this principle. The agent equivalent: filed items are records, and only an explicit selection makes them work.
- **"~" and the Chowder threshold as agent rules.** Discovered work is recorded on the item as a nice-to-have by default and is not done. A loose follow-up list longer than 3 to 5 entries signals a separate scope, to be filed as its own item.
- **Hill-chart status for agents.** An agent can report each scope as uphill (unknowns remain) or downhill (execution only). A dot that stays still across checkpoints is a mechanical "raised hand". Agents do not hide uncertainty out of social fear, but they have their own failure: they claim completion early (see Q3, Anthropic harness and Laban et al.). Uphill and downhill claims therefore need evidence ("hands, not head").
- **What assumes humans.** The betting table's judgment. "Important ideas come back", which relies on human memory and lobbying. Cool-down's "work on whatever they want", which for an agent is exactly the scope creep to avoid. Agents record everything reliably, so the natural forgetting that makes "let it go" work never happens. A filed-item store for agents needs explicit expiry, or a recurrence count that stands in for "it came back".

### Gaps
- The fetched Shape Up pages did not show the author or publication year. The book is commonly attributed to Ryan Singer (Basecamp, 2019), but that was not confirmed from fetched text.
- Chapter 5 does not use the term "no-gos". It appears in Chapter 6 (pitch ingredients) and as fencing language in Chapter 5.
- Chapter 10 (Hand Over Responsibility), which holds the full imagined-vs-discovered-tasks discussion, was not fetched. Only Chapter 12's summary and the glossary were read.
- No published account of Shape Up applied to AI agents was searched for or found.

## 2. Spec-driven development for agents: GitHub Spec Kit, AWS Kiro, OpenSpec, Harper Reed. How do they fix scope before code, and what happens to out-of-scope work?

### Takeaway
All four fix scope before code in the same way. They separate what and why from how. They force unknowns into explicit markers instead of guesses. They put a review between phases. They break work into small tasks that can be checked off. Only OpenSpec has a first-class "Out of scope" list. The strongest scope fence for fixes is Kiro's Bugfix Spec, whose "Unchanged Behavior" section states what must keep working. None of the tools ships a mechanism that routes work discovered during implementation into a new, separate change. Every tool lets you update artifacts mid-stream, which is a scope-expansion path unless those updates are reviewed.

### Cited Findings
**GitHub Spec Kit** (latest release v1.2.3, published 2026-10-22 per the GitHub releases API)
- The README describes Spec Kit as "an open source toolkit that gives AI coding agents structured processes, reusable templates, and documented outcomes." The current minimal flow is `/speckit-constitution`, `/speckit-specify`, `/speckit-plan`, `/speckit-tasks`, `/speckit-implement`, `/speckit-converge`, with steps run "one at a time, reviewing each result before continuing". The README also says "Repeat implement and converge until convergence reports `Converged`." Clarify, checklist and consistency analysis are documented in the SDD walkthrough. — [Spec Kit README](https://github.com/github/spec-kit); [raw README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/spec-kit/main/README.md); [releases API](https://api.github.com/repos/github/spec-kit/releases/latest)
- The README lists an "Idea assessment" workflow that yields "An evidence-backed go, clarify, or stop decision". Extensions exist (for example `specify extension add bug`), and hooks can "Run extension commands before or after Spec Kit commands." — [raw README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/spec-kit/main/README.md); [Spec Kit README](https://github.com/github/spec-kit)
- Unknowns are marked "[NEEDS CLARIFICATION: specific question]" under the rule "Don't guess: If the prompt doesn't specify something, mark it." A spec is complete only when "No [NEEDS CLARIFICATION] markers remain". — [spec-driven.md](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/spec-driven.md)
- Template rules: "Focus on WHAT users need and WHY", "Avoid HOW to implement", "No speculative or 'might need' features", and "Every feature must trace back to a concrete user story with clear acceptance criteria." — [spec-driven.md](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/spec-driven.md)
- The constitution's "Phase -1 gates" are checkbox questions such as "Using ≤3 projects?" and "No future-proofing?". The model cannot proceed unless it passes them or records a justified exception under "Complexity Tracking". Amending the constitution requires "Review and approval by project maintainers" and "Explicit documentation of the rationale for change." — [spec-driven.md](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/spec-driven.md)
- In the spec template, user stories are prioritised (P1, P2 and so on). Each must be "INDEPENDENTLY TESTABLE", meaning that "if you implement just ONE of them, you should still have a viable MVP". Scope boundaries go under "Assumptions", with the example "Mobile support is out of scope for v1". Success criteria "must be technology-agnostic and measurable." — [spec-template.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/spec-kit/main/templates/spec-template.md)
- Change is handled by editing the spec, which is treated as "the source of truth, with code as the generated output". "Maintaining software means evolving specifications." — [spec-driven.md](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/spec-driven.md)

**AWS Kiro specs** (Specs page updated 2026-10-15; Bugfix Specs page updated 2026-08-04)
- A spec has three files: `requirements.md` (or `bugfix.md`), `design.md` and `tasks.md`. The phases run Requirements or Bug Analysis, then Design, then Tasks. Spec types are Feature Specs (Requirements-First or Design-First), Bugfix Specs, and Quick Spec. Quick Spec generates all three files "without approval gates". A Review button lets the user comment on exact passages in each phase. Tasks run individually or "all at once" in dependency-ordered waves. — [Kiro Specs](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/)
- `bugfix.md` has three sections. Current Behavior: "WHEN [condition] THEN the system [incorrect behavior]". Expected Behavior: "...SHALL [correct behavior]". Unchanged Behavior (Regression Prevention): "WHEN [condition] THEN the system SHALL CONTINUE TO [existing behavior]". This "ensures Kiro understands not just what's broken, but what must remain working." — [Kiro Bugfix Specs](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/bugfix-specs/)
- Kiro lists "Scoped fixes" as a benefit: "Explicit constraints ensure only necessary changes are made". It asks the user for "Any constraints (code that shouldn't be modified)". It recommends Bugfix Specs for "Situations where previous fix attempts caused regressions". Its tests check three things: the bug reproduces, the bug is fixed, and nothing regresses. — [Kiro Bugfix Specs](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/bugfix-specs/)

**OpenSpec (Fission-AI)** (latest release v1.15.0, published 2026-10-24 per the GitHub releases API)
- OpenSpec is "a lightweight spec layer" that gets agreement on what to build before code. The default commands are `/opsx:explore` (a "no-stakes thinking partner"), `/opsx:propose`, `/opsx:apply` and `/opsx:archive`. You "review the plan before any code is written". Its philosophy is "fluid not rigid", and you can "update any artifact anytime" with "no rigid phase gates". It advises you to "clear your context before starting implementation." — [OpenSpec README](https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec); [releases API](https://api.github.com/repos/Fission-AI/OpenSpec/releases/latest)
- `proposal.md` "captures **intent**, **scope**, and **approach**". The example has "In scope:" and "Out of scope:" lists, with "Custom color themes (future work)" under out of scope. A proposal is updated on "Scope changes (narrowing or expanding)". Delta specs use `## ADDED`, `## MODIFIED` and `## REMOVED Requirements`. Archiving applies each delta to the main spec. Tasks should be "small enough to complete in one session". Each change folder is "self-contained", and "Multiple changes can exist simultaneously without conflicting." — [OpenSpec concepts.md](https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec/blob/main/docs/concepts.md)

**Harper Reed, "My LLM codegen workflow atm" (2025-02-16)**
- Step one is idea honing: "Ask me one question at a time so we can develop a thorough, step-by-step spec for this idea", which produces `spec.md`. A reasoning model then builds a blueprint of small iterative steps, "small enough to be implemented safely with strong testing" with "no big jumps in complexity at any stage", saved as `prompt_plan.md` and `todo.md`. Each step must be wired into earlier ones, so there is no orphaned code. Checking off `todo.md` "keeps state across sessions". — [harper.blog](https://harper.blog/2025/02/16/my-llm-codegen-workflow-atm/)
- When the model races ahead ("over my skis"), his remedies are the plan document, tests, and stepping away. For existing code he plans per task, gives the model one issue at a time, and uses a new branch per issue. — [harper.blog](https://harper.blog/2025/02/16/my-llm-codegen-workflow-atm/)

### Inferences
- **The shared mechanism** is a written contract for scope (spec, proposal or bugfix analysis), approved before code, then executed as small tasks that can be checked off. The agent's freedom is limited to the "how" inside an approved "what".
- **The best fit for troubleshooting is Kiro's Bugfix Spec shape:** current behaviour, expected behaviour, unchanged behaviour, plus "code that shouldn't be modified". In the 31-to-32-file case, the approved file set belongs in the unchanged/constraint section. Adding a 32nd file then plainly violates the contract and must become a new change, not a revision of the current one.
- **Out-of-scope work.** OpenSpec's self-contained change folders and its "Out of scope: … (future work)" list are the closest thing to "file it, don't do it". A discovered need becomes a new change folder (proposal only) rather than an edit to the current one. Spec Kit's "No speculative or 'might need' features" and its "Complexity Tracking" exception log are the other halves: forbid by default, and justify exceptions in writing.
- **Loophole.** Spec Kit treats the spec as the source of truth, and OpenSpec allows editing any artifact anytime. Unless a scope edit itself requires review, an agent can widen scope by rewriting the spec mid-fix. For autonomous lines, a rule such as "edits that widen In-scope or remove an Unchanged entry route to the item owner" closes it.
- **What assumes humans.** Every phase gate assumes a person reads and approves each artifact (Kiro's Review button, Spec Kit's "reviewing each result", Reed's interview and "take a walk"). In an autonomous line that gate must become a mechanical check (the diff touches only declared files or requirements) or an independent reviewer. Spec Kit's Idea assessment ("go, clarify, or stop") is a model for a research or backlog item's output: a decision, not a prototype.

### Gaps
- The Spec Kit README and spec-driven.md show no explicit out-of-scope mechanism beyond the Assumptions example and the anti-speculation rule. The "evolving existing specs" guide and the SDD walkthrough (clarify, analyze, checklist) were not fetched.
- The semantics of `/speckit-converge` were not fetched beyond the README line.
- The Kiro Specs page does not mention EARS notation by name, although the bugfix syntax (WHEN/THEN/SHALL) follows that pattern.
- No independent evaluation was found of whether spec-driven tools reduce scope creep or file counts. None was searched in depth.

## 3. Research on goal drift in LLM agents: causes and mitigations (restating goals, explicit goal checks, context resets)

### Takeaway
Measured goal drift in agents comes mainly from in-context pattern-matching and momentum. Agents keep doing what the recent trajectory has been doing, including trajectories they inherit from earlier or weaker agents. Competing pressures in the environment and the length of instrumental work make it worse. Strong goal statements reduce drift measurably. Recitation (rewriting a to-do or goal list into recent context), external structured state, one task per session and fresh contexts are practitioner mitigations with no controlled evidence yet. No study measures "spawning new work items" as a form of drift: the benchmarks test value conflicts, not task expansion.

### Cited Findings
- **Arike, Donoway, Bartsch, Hobbhahn, "Evaluating Goal Drift in Language Model Agents"** (arXiv 2505.02709, 5 May 2025). In a simulated stock-trading environment, an agent with a system goal (for example minimising emissions) first pursues an instrumental goal, then faces adversarial pressure: news, stakeholder emails and distracting requests that never directly instruct a goal change. "all evaluated models exhibit some degree of goal drift". The best agent, a scaffolded Claude 3.5 Sonnet, kept "nearly perfect goal adherence" past 100,000 tokens in the hardest setting. Drift correlates with "increasing susceptibility to pattern-matching behaviors as the context length grows". — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02709); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02709v1)
- Further details from the same paper. Strong goal elicitation reduced drift for all models (p < 0.05). GPT-4o mini drifted substantially after only 16 instrumental steps. Drift through inaction (failing to undo off-goal positions) generally exceeded drift through action. Models' stated goals tracked their drift scores. Replacing the instrumental phase with a repeated token produced less drift, so raw token distance is not the main driver. Goal switching (breaking from in-context patterns) drifted more than pressure alone. The authors' stated limitations: a single simple environment, binary goals, explicit pressure, simple scaffolds, and no reasoning models tested. The body does not describe the scaffold. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02709v1)
- **Menon et al., "Inherited Goal Drift: Contextual Pressure Can Undermine Agentic Goals"** (arXiv 2603.03258, 3 Mar 2026, ICLR 2026 Lifelong Agents Workshop). Models are mostly robust to adversarial pressure, but "the same models often inherit drift when conditioned on prefilled trajectories from weaker agents". Only "GPT-5.1 maintaining consistent resilience among tested models". Drift correlates poorly with how well a model follows an instruction hierarchy. There is "preliminary evidence" of transfer to an emergency-room triage environment. The authors call for "refined post-training techniques to mitigate this." — [arXiv 2603.03258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03258)
- **Li et al., "Measuring and Controlling Instruction (In)Stability in Language Model Dialogs"** (arXiv 2402.10962, COLM 2024). The paper reports "significant instruction drift within eight rounds of conversations" for LLaMA2-chat-70B and GPT-3.5. It attributes this partly to attention decay over long exchanges and proposes split-softmax as a mitigation. — [arXiv 2402.10962](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10962)
- **Laban et al., "LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation"** (arXiv 2505.06120, 9 May 2025). Performance drops 39% on average when instructions are spread across turns, mostly through "a significant increase in unreliability". Models "prematurely attempt to generate final solutions" and, once off track, "they get lost and do not recover." — [arXiv 2505.06120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)
- **Wen et al., "Aligning Progress and Feasibility: A Neuro-Symbolic Dual Memory Framework for Long-Horizon LLM Agents"** (arXiv 2604.02734, 3 Apr 2026). The paper names "Progress Drift" (agents "deviate from the main objective") as the global failure mode. It counters it with a Progress Memory distilled from successful trajectories plus symbolic feasibility checks, and reports lower invalid-action rates and shorter trajectories on ALFWorld, WebShop and TextCraft. The abstract gives no figures. — [arXiv 2604.02734](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02734)
- **Practitioner report: recitation.** Manus agents keep a `todo.md` and rewrite it: "By constantly rewriting the todo list, Manus is reciting its objectives into the end of the context". The purpose is to counter lost-in-the-middle effects over "around 50 tool calls on average". The post also argues for keeping errors in context: "Erasing failure removes evidence." (Yichao Ji, 18 Jul 2025.) — [Manus blog](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)
- **Practitioner report: harness structure (Anthropic, 26 Nov 2025).** Observed failure modes: the agent tried to "try to do too much at once", and later sessions would "declare the job done" early. Mitigations: a feature list in JSON where agents may only flip a `passes` flag ("It is unacceptable to remove or edit tests because this could lead to missing or buggy functionality"), a progress file, one feature per session (described as critical to curbing overreach), and git commits for recovery. — [Anthropic, Effective harnesses for long-running agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)
- **Practitioner report: context management (Anthropic, 29 Sep 2025).** "Context rot": as context grows, "the model's ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases". Mitigations are compaction (summarise and restart, preserving decisions and open bugs), structured notes outside the context (for example NOTES.md and to-do lists, reloaded after resets), and sub-agents that start with clean contexts and return condensed summaries of roughly 1,000 to 2,000 tokens. — [Anthropic, Effective context engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)

### Inferences
- **Causes that map to the operator's failures.** (a) Trajectory inheritance and pattern-matching: an agent that resumes old research studies is conditioned on those studies' momentum, and Menon et al. show that prefilled trajectories transmit drift even to robust models. (b) Environmental pressure: during troubleshooting, findings that look like work play the role of the "distracting requests" in Arike et al. (c) Premature solutions with no recovery (Laban) look like an agent committing early to "build a prototype".
- **Mitigations, ordered by strength of evidence.** (1) Strong, explicit goal statements: measured, p < 0.05, in one environment. (2) Scaffolding: measured but not described. (3) Asking the agent to state its current goal as a drift detector: inferred from the finding that stated goals tracked drift; this is a correlation in one environment, not a tested control. (4) Recitation, external progress files, one feature per session, fresh or sub-agent contexts: practitioner reports only.
- **Drift through inaction matters.** An agent can fail the North Star by not finishing the actual fix while it is busy elsewhere. Goal checks should therefore ask "is the original problem resolved yet?", not just "was this action on-goal?".
- **Context resets cut both ways.** A fresh context removes inherited momentum (good), but it also drops evidence of earlier failures (the Manus caution). A reset should carry the goal, the scope fence and the error log forward, and leave the old trajectory behind.
- **What transfers.** Everything in this section is agent-native. The human analogues (fatigue rotation, handoff documents) appear in Q4.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures scope expansion, such as spawning follow-up items or growing a change's file set, as a form of drift. The benchmarks use value-conflict goals (profit against emissions, triage priorities).
- The scaffold behind Claude 3.5 Sonnet's robustness is not described in the body of Arike et al.
- No controlled evidence was found that restating goals or resetting context reduces drift in coding or troubleshooting agents. That evidence is practitioner-reported only.
- "LongHorizon-Harness" (alphaXiv 2608.01964, Aug 2026) appeared in search results. Its snippet described errors accumulating along a trajectory and steering the agent away from its objective, with explicit external task state as the remedy. It was not fetched, so its content is not cited.
- The verbatim abstract of 2505.02709 was not reproduced because of fetch-tool quoting limits. The summary above comes from the abstract and HTML pages.

## 4. Incident-response discipline that bounds troubleshooting: Google SRE and ICS, PagerDuty, the XP spike. How does each separate "restore service now" from "deeper fix later"?

### Takeaway
Incident disciplines bound troubleshooting with roles and objectives, not willpower. One commander owns the objectives and the scope, and does not fix anything. Only a designated operations role changes the system. A planning or scribe role files bugs and follow-ups for later. Mitigation comes before root cause. Tasks are assigned to one named owner and timeboxed with a check-back. Objectives are restated per operational period. The XP spike is the knowledge-only counterpart: build only what answers the question under examination, ignore everything else, and expect to throw the code away.

### Inferences
- **How "now" and "later" are separated:** (1) Different owners. The person who decides objectives does not fix. The fixer does not decide scope. Planning or the Scribe captures the "later" work. (2) A different time. Deeper fixes become postmortem action items that leads schedule. (3) A different artifact. Bugs and TODO notes are filed, not executed. In an agent line, the troubleshooting worker would have a "file follow-up" channel and no mandate to act on what it files.
- **"Only Ops modifies the system" is the rule the 31-to-32-file case broke.** The fixing agent acted as both Ops and Planning, and widened the change set itself. Under ICS-style rules, a change outside the stated objective goes to the commander as a proposal ("Collect proposed repair actions … recommend").
- **The IAP as a per-checkpoint scope statement.** At each checkpoint (the agent's "operational period"), restate the objective and compare results against it. New objectives are added only at a period boundary, and only by the owner. This is the human version of the goal recitation in Q3.
- **Mitigation-first gives a clean test:** "Does this action restore the stated behaviour now?" If not, it is root-cause or prevention work and gets filed. "You only need to know the location of the root cause" to mitigate, so a deep investigation is not a precondition for the fix.
- **The spike fits research items.** A research or study item's output is knowledge (a decision or a report), and any code is throwaway. A prototype that grows out of a study is new production scope and should be a separate, deliberately selected item. That is the boundary the backlog-route studies crossed.
- **Timeboxed tasks with check-back**, translated for advisory-budget settings: the timebox is a scheduled checkpoint, not a kill. At the check-back, an owner decides whether to continue, re-shape or stop. "We can either get you that list, or fix the incident. Not both" is a ready-made refusal pattern for tangents.
- **What assumes humans.** Voice-call choreography, polling for strong objections, the bystander effect, and fatigue rotation every four hours. The agent analogue of rotation is a fresh context, handed off through the live state document (Q3: context rot, compaction).

## 5. North Star metrics and OKRs as decision filters; parking-lot and "not now" lists

### Takeaway
A North Star and OKRs work as filters only once they are made concrete enough to test a task against: a small number of objectives, measurable key results or inputs, and published commitments, so that "no" points at a prior commitment rather than at a person. Parking lots capture off-scope items without acting on them, but they need owners and follow-up or they become graveyards. Shape Up takes the opposite position: let unchosen ideas go, because important ones come back. Agents capture everything perfectly, so an agent line needs a deliberate rule between those two poles.

### Cited Findings
- **Amplitude, North Star Metric.** A good one "represents the value users get from your product", lies within product and marketing's influence, leads revenue, and is actionable and measurable. It is driven by three to five inputs that teams "directly influence with their day-to-day work". "If you can move your North Star directly, it's probably not a good North Star" (John Cutler). "when teams align their activities to your North Star, they deliver work that furthers your strategy." The vanity metrics to avoid include page views, downloads and "story points delivered". (Julia Sholtz, originally published 2024-08-15, page dated 2026-07-15.) — [Amplitude blog](https://amplitude.com/blog/good-bad-north-star-metric)
- Amplitude's playbook frames the purpose as: "Bringing coherence to the work of everyone in your organization is what it's all about." — [Amplitude North Star Playbook intro](https://amplitude.com/books/north-star/about-the-north-star-framework)
- **Google re:Work, OKRs.** Objectives "are ambitious and may feel somewhat uncomfortable". Key results "are measurable and should be easy to grade with a number". Use "just three to five objectives" with "around three key results per objective". The grading sweet spot is 60–70%. OKRs are public. Once objectives are agreed, declining lower-priority requests becomes easier, because refusal becomes a response to an organisational commitment rather than a personal dispute. OKRs are not a to-do list: they define impact and leave methods to the team. — [re:Work: Set goals with OKRs](https://rework.withgoogle.com/en/guides/set-goals-with-okrs)
- **NN/g, parking lots** (Sarah Gibbons, 8 Sep 2019, reviewed 12 Dec 2024). "A parking lot is a place to capture comments, topics, or questions that are not related to the meeting agenda." It "should be collaborative and visible to everyone". Off-scope items are flagged immediately. Gray-area topics are time-boxed: if the discussion runs over, "move it to the parking lot and ask the key contributors to finish the conversation after the meeting." Each parked item gets "W.W.W" (who, what, when), and you should "Always include the parking lot in meeting followups". Without follow-up it "can quickly become a graveyard of questions you don't want to deal with." — [NN/g Parking Lots](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parking-lots/)
- **Shape Up's counter-position:** "Interesting. Maybe some day." ([Ch. 3](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03)); personal lists are not "direct inputs to the betting process", and unchosen ideas: "we let it go" ([Ch. 7](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.1-chapter-07)).
- **Incident-response "not now" lists:** the Scribe's "follow-ups slated for later" ([PagerDuty](https://response.pagerduty.com/during/during_an_incident/)); the Planning role "filing bugs" ([SRE book](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/)).
- **Spec "not now" lists:** OpenSpec's "Out of scope: … (future work)" ([concepts.md](https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec/blob/main/docs/concepts.md)); Spec Kit's "No speculative or 'might need' features" ([spec-driven.md](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/spec-driven.md)); Shape Up's "~" nice-to-haves that "usually … never get built" ([Ch. 14](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.5-chapter-14)).

### Inferences
**A per-task filter assembled from these sources.** Each check is an inference that combines the cited mechanisms.
1. **Objective test (OKR, ICS management by objectives):** Does this action move the item's stated objective or acceptance criterion? If not, park it.
2. **Must-have test (Shape Up hammering):** Can the fix ship without it? If yes, mark it "~" or file it.
3. **Fence test (no-gos, Kiro Unchanged Behavior):** Does it touch anything declared out of scope or unchanged, such as a new file in an approved set? If yes, file a proposal and do not act.
4. **Spawn test (bets, not backlogs):** Would it create a new item, prototype or approval? If yes, it is a bet for the owner, not something the worker does.
5. **Mitigation test (SRE):** Is it needed to restore the stated behaviour now, or is it root cause or prevention? If the latter, it becomes a follow-up.

**Making "no" cheap.** re:Work's point that refusal is easy when it cites a public commitment transfers directly. An agent's filed item can cite the North Star line or item objective it would have diverted from.

**North Star altitude.** A North Star Metric is product-level and deliberately "not directly movable". It must be decomposed into per-item objectives (inputs, or key results) before an agent can test a task against it. A bare North Star in a prompt is too abstract to act as a filter.

**The parking lot's failure mode reverses for agents.** Humans let parking lots rot into graveyards. Agents fill them at volume and may treat them as a work queue, as in the backlog route. A balanced rule would keep both ends: capture every off-scope item (the NN/g pole), never treat the lot as a dispatch input (the Shape Up pole), and promote an item only by deliberate selection, with recurrence counts standing in for "important ideas come back".

**What assumes humans.** OKR stretch grading (60–70%) exists to motivate people. Agent items are better treated as committed objectives. Parking-lot social cues (dismissiveness, participation) do not apply to agents.

**Mapping the operator's two examples:**
- *Backlog route leading to old studies, then new prototypes.* This violates Bets-not-backlogs: the backlog became a direct input. It violates the spike rule: a study produced production scope instead of knowledge. And it shows inherited trajectory drift (Menon et al.). Countermeasures: backlog items are records, not dispatch inputs; a study's output is a decision or report ("go, clarify, or stop" in Spec Kit's Idea assessment); any prototype is a new, deliberately selected item.
- *A fix that grew a 31-file publication to 32 files.* This violates a fence: the approved file set should be an explicit no-go or Unchanged constraint (Kiro: "code that shouldn't be modified"), and "only Ops modifies the system" within the stated objective (SRE). Countermeasures: declare the approved set in the item; any change outside it becomes a filed proposal, and the circuit breaker applies (close and re-shape, do not extend the item).
